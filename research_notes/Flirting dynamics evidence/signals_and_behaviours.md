# Flirting behaviours and signals: what people do, and what goes with attraction or interest being recognised

Evidence labels: **strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **moderate** (several studies, often one lab, or one good trial), **mixed** (replications disagree), **weak** (one small study, secondary report, or expert opinion), **myth** (tested and not supported).

Method note: about 19 tool calls. Several primary PDFs (UCI-hosted McFarland 2013 and Ranganath 2013, KU news, NatCom) could not be fetched (DNS failures), so many details come from abstracts, university press releases and search-result summaries. These are marked "secondary" where relevant. No claim below comes from memory without a link; recalled details I could not verify are listed under Gaps.

## 1. Nonverbal courtship signals (gaze, smiling, proximity, touch, posture, laughter)

### Takeaway
The best evidence is a 2018 meta-analysis (Montoya, Kershaw & Prosser, *Psychological Bulletin*). It finds that being closer, making more eye contact, nodding, smiling and laughing go with self-reported attraction, but the effects are small (r ≈ .13–.21). These cues do not tell romantic interest apart from friendly liking. Many popular "tells" (hair flips, head tilts, open posture, leaning in) showed no link. Moore's 1985 catalogue of 52 female solicitation signals is a foundational observational study rather than a replicated finding.

### Cited Findings
- **[strong; small effects]** Montoya, Kershaw & Prosser (2018), "A meta-analytic investigation of the relation between interpersonal attraction and enacted behavior," *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7), 673–709. — [Murdoch research portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analytic-investigation-of-the-relation/991005568569707891)
  - Nonverbal correlates of attraction: decreased proximity r = .207, frequency of eye contact r = .189, nodding r = .162, smiling r = .131 (secondary press summary). — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-indicator-9008/); [University of Alberta Folio](https://ualberta.ca/folio/2018/09/how-to-tell-if-someone-likes-you.html)
  - Eye contact, smiling, laughter and starting conversations were linked to attraction "across most world cultures" (press coverage). — [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/500760/how-can-you-tell-if-someone-likes-you-indicators-of-attraction/)
  - The associations were about the same size for romantic interest and for platonic/social attraction. So these cues signal liking but cannot tell flirting from friendliness, and the lead author said the behaviours have "little or nothing to do with romantic desires" (secondary). — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-indicator-9008/); [NatCom summary](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/why-many-tell-tale-signs-attraction-are-likely-nonsense/)
  - **[myth in this meta-analysis]** Hair flipping, eyebrow raising, gesturing, head tilting, primping clothes, open body posture and leaning in showed no evidence of being related to liking. — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-indicator-9008/); [IFLScience](https://www.iflscience.com/editors-blog/these-are-the-signs-that-someone-is-attracted-to-you-according-to-a-new-study/)
  - Caveat: sources give different study counts (press: 54 papers; NatCom commentary: 138 studies of high romantic-intensity interactions and 148 of heterosexual dyads), probably reflecting different subsets. — [NatCom](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/why-many-tell-tale-signs-attraction-are-likely-nonsense/)
- **[weak/moderate: foundational, observational, not directly replicated]** Moore (1985), *Ethology and Sociobiology*: two trained observers covertly watched more than 200 single women in bars and on a campus (about 30 minutes each) and catalogued 52 "nonverbal solicitation signals", e.g. the short darting glance and the hair flip. Women who signalled more were approached more often, with a reported ~4x rate in secondary accounts. A summary of Moore's later work says signalling predicted being approached better than physical attractiveness did (secondary; Psychology Today). — [Psychology Today: The Attraction Doctor](https://www.psychologytoday.com:443/us/blog/the-attraction-doctor/201706/11-key-flirting-techniques-women)
  - Moore (2010) reviewed nonverbal courtship research in the *Journal of Sex Research*, drawing on both observation and self-report. — [JCI Insight reference listing](https://insight.jci.org/references/scholar/2295/B66)
  - Note the tension: the hair flip is a Moore signal, but the 2018 meta-analysis found no link between hair flips and liking. Moore's signals predict being approached in bars, which is not the same outcome as self-reported attraction.
- **[moderate, one lab]** Grammer, Kruck, Juette & Fink (2000), *Evolution and Human Behavior*: videotaped encounters between opposite-sex strangers suggest that women initiate and steer these interactions. Courtship-like behaviour was most frequent in the first minute but only correlated with women's stated interest from about minute 4 to minute 10, so early signals were not reliable indicators of interest. — [Göttingen publication page](https://psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grammeretal2000); [Univ. Vienna project page](https://evolution.anthro.univie.ac.at/projects/human%20motion/Female%20Control%20Behaviour/index.html)
  - Related: Grammer, Kruck & Magnusson (1998), "The courtship dance: patterns of non-verbal synchronization in opposite-sex encounters," *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* 22(1), 3–29. I have only the citation, not its findings. — [Univ. Vienna page](https://evolution.anthro.univie.ac.at/projects/human%20motion/Female%20Control%20Behaviour/index.html)
- **[weak/mixed: single-author field studies, integrity concerns]** Touch: Guéguen (2007, *Social Influence*), field experiments in France. A 1–2 s forearm touch raised acceptance of a dance invitation from 43% to 65% and street phone-number compliance from 10% to 19% (BPS Research Digest, secondary). — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/power-light-touch-arm); [Stafforini summary](https://stafforini.com/works/gueguen-2007-courtship-compliance-effect/)
  - In 2023 SAGE issued expressions of concern about several Guéguen papers. The courtship-touch paper is not reported on that list, but his body of work as a whole is under scrutiny. — [Improbable Research, "Guéguen Busted?"](https://improbable.com/2023/03/18/gueguen-busted)
  - Guéguen & Joule's 2008 meta-analysis (in French) on touch and compliance concluded that the effect is real but depends on the outcome measured. — [Sciences Po Lyon listing](https://signal.sciencespo-lyon.fr/article/560138)
- **[moderate, one lab]** Detecting flirting is hard. Hall, Xing & Brooks (2014/2015), *Communication Research* 42(7), 939–958, studied 52 opposite-sex stranger dyads (N = 104) who talked for 10–12 minutes. Interactions with no flirting were judged more accurately than ones with flirting, i.e. people tend to miss flirting. Female targets' flirting was judged more accurately than male targets'. — [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/65a73d2d-0834-497c-a26e-5aadf7cf0b4a); [KU News 2015](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2015/02/12/just-time-valentines-day-research-defines-more-behaviors-reveal-romantic-attraction)
- **[moderate, one lab]** Hall & Xing (2015), "The verbal and nonverbal correlates of the five flirting styles," *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior* 39, 41–68: different verbal and nonverbal cues indicated physical attraction depending on the person's flirting style, so no single universal tell was found. — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/nonverbal-communication-attraction-psychology-1759/)

### Inferences
- Proximity, eye contact, smiling, nodding and laughter are the defensible "signs of liking", but only as weak probabilistic cues. They are not diagnostic of romantic intent, and observers tend to miss real flirting rather than see flirting that isn't there.
- Moore's bar signals and the hair flip should be described as observed female solicitation behaviours. They are not validated indicators of attraction, and the meta-analysis does not support the hair flip, head tilt or open posture as signs of liking.
- Cultural limits: most primary studies are US, European or French, often student samples. The meta-analysis press release claims cross-cultural consistency, but I could not check its moderator analyses.

### Gaps
- No Duchenne-smile-specific courtship study was found in my searches. Montoya's "smiling" does not separate Duchenne from non-Duchenne smiles.
- Givens's courtship-phase model (attention, recognition, interaction, arousal, resolution) is expert opinion/ethology. I found no URL for it in this round, so it is unsourced here and should be treated as **weak**.
- I could not open the Moore 1985 primary text or find a formal replication. A Moore 1995 adolescent study is mentioned in secondary sources but was not verified.
- Effect sizes for laughter and talk-initiation in Montoya 2018 were not retrieved.

## 2. Verbal and vocal features in speed dates (McFarland, Jurafsky & Rawlings; Ranganath et al.)

### Takeaway
In about 1,000 four-minute Stanford speed dates, women reported "clicking" with men who expressed appreciation and sympathy and who interrupted collaboratively, for example by finishing or adding to her sentence. Both sexes reported clicking more when the conversation centred on the woman. Machine models detect intended flirtation only modestly (about 59% on new speakers vs 48% baseline), and humans are poor at perceiving a partner's intended flirtation because they project their own intent onto the partner.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate: one large corpus, one lab, not independently replicated]** McFarland, Jurafsky & Rawlings (2013), "Making the Connection: Social Bonding in Courtship Situations," *American Journal of Sociology* 118(6), 1596–1649. Nearly 1,000 audio-recorded four-minute speed dates among Stanford graduate students. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click); [paper PDF (UCI-hosted)](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)
  - Women reported feeling connected to men who used appreciative language and sympathy/empathy. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click)
  - Women felt connected when men interrupted to show engagement (finishing or adding to her sentence) rather than to change the topic. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click)
  - Both sexes reported clicking when the conversation was mainly about the woman. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click); [Scientific American podcast](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/speed-dating-study-yields-conversat-13-05-14)
  - The paper won the AJS Roger V. Gould Prize. — [Stanford GSE](https://ed.stanford.edu/node/5675)
- **[moderate, same corpus]** Ranganath, Jurafsky & McFarland. The 2009 NAACL paper reports a system detecting a speaker's *intent* to flirt with up to 71.5% accuracy, better than the human conversation partners. — [ACL Anthology N09-1072](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/N09-1072.pdf)
  - The 2013 *Computer Speech and Language* version, "Detecting friendly, flirtatious, awkward, and assertive speech in speed-dates," used lexical, prosodic and dialogue features on the top 10% of each stance. It reached 75% accuracy on speakers seen in training (50% baseline) but only 59% on new speakers (48% baseline). — [PDF (UCI-hosted)](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)
  - Humans are "very poor perceivers of intended flirtatiousness" and tend to project their own intended behaviour onto the partner. Flirtation went with joint focus on the woman. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf); [Stanford Magazine "Crossed Wires"](https://stanfordmag.org/contents/crossed-wires)
  - Corpus size is reported as 991 (2009), "over 1000" (2013), and 946 (Stanford news). — [ACL 2009](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/N09-1072.pdf); [Stanford news](https://ed.stanford.edu/node/1819)
- **[weak, secondary]** Mimicry: a BPS Research Digest item is titled "Mimicry improves women's speed-dating success". I could not read the underlying study. — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/mimicry-improves-womens-speed-dating-success)

### Inferences
- Practical message: responsive listening (appreciation, sympathy, collaborative completion, focus on her) goes with felt connection in heterosexual speed dates. Topic-changing interruptions do not.
- The low generalisation of the detector to new speakers (59% vs 48%) and the projection finding suggest that flirtation is idiosyncratic and that people infer it from their own intent. This fits the Hall et al. finding that people miss flirting.
- Limits: one elite US graduate-student sample, heterosexual dyads, four-minute dates, self-reported clicking. There is no independent replication on another corpus.

### Gaps
- Specific prosodic findings (e.g. pitch rises, loudness, laughter rates, use of "you" or "I" by flirting men and women) are in the Ranganath and McFarland PDFs, which I could not fetch. I recall that men's laughter and pitch features were reported, but this is unverified, so it is not stated as fact.
- I found no non-US replication of speech-based "click" predictors.

## 3. Playfulness and teasing

### Takeaway
Teasing is well theorised as "playful provocation" signalled by off-record markers such as an exaggerated tone or a wink (Keltner et al. 2001 review). It can be affiliative or hostile, but direct evidence that teasing increases attraction is thin. Adult playfulness, humour and being "fun-loving" rank highly among desired partner traits in two student surveys (US and German-speaking).

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate as a review; weak for a flirting-specific claim]** Keltner, Capps, Kring, Young & Heerey (2001), "Just Teasing: A Conceptual Analysis and Empirical Review," *Psychological Bulletin* 127(2), 229–248. It defines a tease as a playful provocation commenting on something relevant to the target, which can lead to hostile or affiliative outcomes. — [Berkeley PDF](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf); [Bangor record](https://research.bangor.ac.uk/en/publications/just-teasing-a-conceptual-analysis-and-empirical-review/)
  - Off-record markers (exaggerated tone, wink) signal non-literal intent (secondary commentary). Teasing is cited as a way to express affection and resolve conflict with romantic partners (Psychology Today summary, secondary). — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/the-wide-wide-world-of-psychology/201311/when-does-teasing-go-too-far); [Psychology in Action](https://www.psychologyinaction.org/2008-12-30-teasing-bullying-or-playful-fun/)
- **[moderate: two samples, two countries, both student self-report]** Chick, Yarnal & Purrington (2012), *American Journal of Play*, surveyed about 254 US undergraduates (164 men, 89 women, ages 18–26). Among 16 traits, "sense of humor," "fun loving" and "playful" ranked 2nd–4th for traits women want in men. The authors caution that stated preferences may not match actual choices. — [Penn State news](https://news.psu.edu/story/147649/2012/08/03/research/playfulness-may-help-adults-attract-mates-study-finds); [Museum of Play PDF](https://www.museumofplay.org/app/uploads/2022/01/7-2-article-playfulness-in-adults-revisited.pdf)
- Proyer & Wagner (2015), *American Journal of Play*: 327 students from Germany, Switzerland and Austria. Friendliness, intelligence, humour and a "fun tendency" were top-rated, and playfulness itself ranked behind these for both sexes. Playful people valued playfulness more in partners (assortative preference). — [UZH news](https://www.news.uzh.ch/en/articles/2015/-verspielte-erwachsene-werden-bei-der-partnerwahl-bevorzugt.html); [Innovations Report](https://innovations-report.com/social-sciences/playful-adults-preferred-in-choice-of-partner)
- **[moderate]** The "playful" flirting style was associated with more dating success in the Flirting Styles Inventory work (see section 6). — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)

### Inferences
- Playfulness and humour are reliably *stated* as desirable. Whether *playful teasing* in an encounter raises attraction has not been directly tested in the sources I found, so that claim should be presented as **weak**. Teasing carries a risk of being read as hostile when the off-record cues are missing.

### Gaps
- No experimental study of flirtatious teasing or banter and attraction was found.
- No 2020s replication of playfulness-preference findings was checked.

## 4. Humour in attraction

### Takeaway
Humour works both as a quality display and as an interest indicator. People produce more humour toward, and rate as funnier, those they are already attracted to (Li et al. 2009). The sex difference in preferences (women value humour *production*, men value humour *appreciation* of their jokes) has replicated (Bressler et al. 2006; Hone et al. 2015). A meta-analysis finds a small-to-moderate male advantage in rated humour production (d = 0.32).

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate: three studies, one team]** Li, Griskevicius, Durante, Jonason, Pasisz & Aumer (2009), "An Evolutionary Perspective on Humor: Sexual Selection or Interest Indication?" *PSPB* 35(7), 923–936. Men and women were both more likely to initiate humour with, and rate as funnier, people they were initially attracted to, so humour signals interest and not only good genes. One study used 46 undergraduates (Business Insider, secondary). — [SMU InK](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/725); [SMU news](https://socsc.smu.edu.sg/news/simple-way-figure-out-if-people-you); [Business Insider PDF](https://news.smu.edu.sg/sites/news.smu.edu.sg/files/newsroom-pdf/BusinessInsider_20120606_1.pdf)
  - The interest-indicator model was later extended to established couples in a daily-diary study. — [SMU InK](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/1474)
- **[strong: original plus independent replication]** Bressler, Martin & Balshine (2006), *Evolution and Human Behavior* 27, 121–130. Men weighted a partner's *appreciation* of their humour more; women weighted *production* and appreciation about equally, and in forced choice picked producers, especially for sexual relationships. — [McMaster PDF](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/Bressler%20et%20al..pdf)
  - Bressler & Balshine (2006): only women evaluating men preferred humorous partners. Humorous people were rated less intelligent and less trustworthy but more socially adept. — [McMaster PDF](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/Bressler%20-%20Balshine.pdf)
  - Hone, Hurwitz & Lieberman (2015), *Evolutionary Psychology*, replicated the result: men prefer women receptive to their humour and women prefer men who produce it. In a budget trade-off, men treated receptivity as a necessity and women treated production as a necessity. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/721d10f1e06846d0a0bd40820fec57af)
- **[moderate: one study, N = 400]** Greengross & Miller (2011), *Intelligence* 39, 188–192. In 200 men and 200 women, general and verbal intelligence predicted humour-production ability (caption funniness), which predicted mating success (e.g. lifetime sexual partners). Men scored higher on average. — [Aberystwyth record](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/humor-ability-reveals-intelligence-predicts-mating-success-and-is/); [PDF](https://www.uniad.org.br/wp-content/uploads/2012/06/Intelligence_20111.pdf)
- **[strong: meta-analysis]** Greengross, Silvia & Nusbaum (2020), *Journal of Research in Personality* 84, 103886. 28 studies, 36 effect sizes, N = 5,057. Men's humour output was rated funnier, d = 0.321, roughly a 60% chance a random man outscores a random woman. Results were robust to moderators, with little evidence of publication bias. About 60% of the data were unpublished. — [Aberystwyth record](https://research.aber.ac.uk/en/publications/sex-differences-in-humor-production-ability-a-meta-analysis/); [SPSP blog](https://spsp.org/news-center/blog/greengross-men-funnier-than-women); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/meta-analysis-of-28-studies-finds-mens-written-humor-output-tends-to-be-funnier-than-womens/)

### Inferences
- In flirting, laughing at someone's jokes and joking with them are both likely signals of interest (Li et al.). The asymmetry (men want their humour appreciated, women value men who produce it) is one of the better-replicated sex differences in this area, though it rests on Western student samples and heterosexual framing.

### Gaps
- No cross-cultural (non-Western) replication of the production/appreciation asymmetry was checked.
- Exact sample sizes for Li et al. Studies 1–3 were not retrieved beyond the n = 46 secondary figure.

## 5. Compliments in romantic contexts

### Takeaway
I found little peer-reviewed evidence specific to compliments in flirting. The best related evidence is indirect: appreciative language predicted women's sense of "clicking" in speed dates (McFarland et al. 2013). There is also a Guéguen-line field study on complimenting before asking women out (secondary press coverage only, and subject to the Guéguen integrity concerns).

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate, indirect]** Appreciation and praise by men predicted women's reports of connection in about 1,000 speed dates. — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click)
- **[weak, secondary]** Discover Magazine covered a field study on whether complimenting a woman before asking her out improves success. I did not read the details or verify the authors. — [Discover Magazine](https://discovermagazine.com/health/should-you-compliment-a-woman-before-asking-her-out-science-weighs-in)

### Inferences
- Appreciation embedded in responsive conversation has better support than stand-alone appearance compliments or "lines".

### Gaps
- No primary compliment-in-courtship study was verified. This topic needs a targeted search (e.g. Kleinke's opening-lines work, Guéguen compliment studies and their integrity status).

## 6. Flirting Styles Inventory (Hall, Carter, Cody & Albright 2010)

### Takeaway
Five styles were found (traditional, physical, sincere, playful, polite) in large online-dating samples (factor analysis N = 5,020, eHarmony users). The physical, sincere and playful styles went with more dating success. The physical and sincere styles went with faster relationship escalation, stronger emotional connection and stronger physical chemistry. The sincere style centres on emotional connection and genuine interest in the other person.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate: large sample, one research group, self-report, cross-sectional]** Hall, Carter, Cody & Albright (2010), "Individual differences in the communication of romantic interest: Development of the Flirting Styles Inventory," *Communication Quarterly* 58, 365–393. eHarmony adult users; factor analyses (N = 5,020) supported five styles. — [ScienceDaily (KU release)](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm); [Goodreads author blog (Hall)](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4456492-development-of-the-flirting-styles-inventory?tab=author)
  - Physical, sincere and playful styles were linked to more dating success. Physical and sincere styles were linked to faster escalation with more emotional connection and physical chemistry. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)
  - Sincere style: emotional connection and genuine interest. Women score higher, but men endorse it too. Relationships formed this way tend to involve emotional and sexual chemistry and to be meaningful. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/fulfillment-at-any-age/201605/do-you-flirt-more-than-you-realize)
  - Women scored higher on every style except playful, but the co-author noted that gender differences within styles were small. — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)
- **[moderate]** Hall & Xing (2015, *JNB*): each style shows different behavioural cues of attraction in lab interactions with strangers. — [Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/nonverbal-communication-attraction-psychology-1759/)
- Psychology Today's online flirting-style quizzes are self-exploration tools, not validated diagnostics. — [Psychology Today test](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/tests/personality/flirting-personality-test-women)

### Inferences
- "Sincere" flirting (showing genuine interest, emotional connection) has the most consistent association with good relationship outcomes. Because the data are self-reported and cross-sectional among people seeking long-term relationships on eHarmony, the evidence is correlational and drawn from a selected sample.

### Gaps
- The exact traditional and polite style outcome associations (reportedly less success or slower escalation) were not verified in the retrieved text.
- No independent replication outside the Hall group or the US was found.

## 7. Purposes of flirting (Henningsen 2004 motives)

### Takeaway
Henningsen (2004, *Sex Roles*) proposed six motives for flirting: sexual, relational, exploring, fun, esteem and instrumental. Many interactions serve several motives at once. Men lean more toward sexual motives and women toward relational ones, which creates scope for miscommunication. Later work in the same line found men higher on sex and exploring motives.

### Cited Findings
- **[moderate: original plus same-line follow-ups; student and worker samples]** Henningsen (2004), "Flirting with meaning: Examining miscommunication in flirting interactions," *Sex Roles*. Six motives: sex, fun, exploring, relational, esteem (boosting one's own esteem) and instrumental (getting something from the other person). — [Wikipedia: Flirting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flirting)
  - Method (secondary): 101 female and 99 male students wrote hypothetical flirtatious conversations and identified the motives. Men were significantly more likely to give sexual motives, and women relational ones. — [Business Insider NL](https://businessinsider.nl/facts-about-flirting-2017-2); [SheThePeople](https://shethepeople.tv/top-stories/opinion/science-of-flirting)
  - Many flirting interactions involve more than one motive. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flirting)
  - A 2008 follow-up applying the motives to workplace flirting (*Journal of Business Communication*) found men higher on sex and exploring motives. — [SAGE: 10.1177/0021943608319390](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021943608319390)
  - Thesis-level follow-up: women and men perceive the exploring and esteem motives of opening lines differently. — [FHSU thesis](https://scholars.fhsu.edu/theses/3021)

### Inferences
- Because flirting is often non-sexual (fun, esteem, exploring), behaviour-based "signs of interest" will produce false positives. This fits the Montoya finding that cues do not separate romantic from platonic liking, and the Ranganath projection finding.

### Gaps
- I did not access the original *Sex Roles* text, so the method details come from secondary sources. Wikipedia misdates it as 2014 in places.
- No cross-cultural tests of the six-motive model were found.
