# Reading (and misreading) romantic interest, escalation, consent, rejection, culture and flirting myths

Evidence labels: **strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **moderate** (several studies, often one lab, or one good trial), **mixed** (replications disagree), **weak** (one small study, secondary report, or expert opinion), **myth** (tested and not supported).

Method note: about 20 searches and fetches. Several primary-source hosts (utexas.edu, olemiss.edu, figshare, spsp.org) failed to resolve from this environment, so some details come from search-result abstracts, university press releases or secondary coverage. These are labelled as such. I could not confirm effect sizes from full texts unless stated.

## 1. Misperception of sexual/romantic interest (Abbey, error management theory, Perilloux et al., accuracy of detecting flirting)

### Takeaway
The finding that men overperceive women's sexual interest, and women underperceive men's, has been replicated many times across designs and countries (US, Norway, Japan). No meta-analysis was found. People are also poor at detecting flirting when it happens, while they are much better at recognising when it is *not* happening.

### Cited Findings
- **Abbey (1982) is the founding lab study (moderate on its own, strong as the start of a replicated line).** Men read more sexual intent into a mixed-sex interaction than women did, and men also attributed more sexuality to the *male* target. Later work keeps finding that men are more likely than women to overperceive sexual interest. Source: secondary summary in a review and in a bias explainer. — [PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2744967); [The Decision Lab (secondary)](https://thedecisionlab.com/bias/sexual-overperception-bias)
- **Error management theory (Haselton & Buss) is the main explanation (theory, not a finding).** The idea is that when one kind of error costs more than the other, judgments bias towards avoiding the costlier one. For men, missing a real sexual opportunity is assumed to cost more than a false alarm. The adaptive explanation is a hypothesis: a Mannheim summary notes that whether evolution actually *causes* the bias remains open. — [The Decision Lab](https://thedecisionlab.com/bias/sexual-overperception-bias); [Uni Mannheim](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/sexuelles-selbstbewusstsein-eine-frage-des-geschlechts)
- **Perilloux, Easton & Buss (2012), *Psychological Science* 23(2):146–151 (moderate).** N = 199 US undergraduates (96 men, 103 women) took part in speed-meeting: 3-minute chats with each of five opposite-sex partners. Repeated interactions let the authors calculate misperception within each person. Women *underestimated* men's sexual interest. Men overperceived women's interest. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/all-it-takes-is-a-smile-for-some-guys.html); [author PDF (could not be fetched from here)](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/buss/files/2015/09/The-Misperception-of-Sexual-Interest.pdf); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797611424162)
- **Individual differences in the same study (moderate, single study).** Three things predicted more overperception by men: a short-term mating orientation (sociosexuality), higher self-rated attractiveness, and the woman's physical attractiveness. — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/all-it-takes-is-a-smile-for-some-guys.html); [EarthSky (secondary)](https://earthsky.org/human-world/evolution-might-favor-men-who-misinterpret-sexual-cues)
- **Cross-cultural replications of Haselton (2003) (strong for the self-report pattern).**
  - Bendixen (2014), *Evolutionary Psychology* 12(5):1004–1021: N = 308 heterosexual Norwegian students, in a more gender-equal country. Women reported being overperceived for sexual interest much more often than underperceived. For men the gap was small. The FORRT replication atlas counts it as a successful replication. — [Bendixen record, NTNU](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2627053?show=full); [FORRT atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/s0092-6566(02)00529-9/); [NTNU news](https://norwegianscitechnews.com/2015/01/shes-not-interested-in-sex-but-he-thinks-she-is/)
  - Hiraishi et al. (2016), Japan: same trend in a less gender-equal society. Japanese women were also more often overperceived for *romantic* interest. — [LEBS article](https://lebs.hbesj.org/index.php/lebs/article/view/lebs.2016.47)
  - Caveat: these replications use recalled events (self-report of being misperceived), not measured accuracy.
- **Hall, Xing & Brooks, "Accurately Detecting Flirting," *Communication Research* 42(7):939–958 (online 2014, issue 2015) (moderate, one lab, two studies).**
  - Study 1: 52 opposite-sex stranger pairs talked for 10–12 minutes and then reported their own and their partner's flirting. According to the KU release, accuracy was over 80% when the partner was *not* flirting. When the partner *was* flirting, 36% of men detected it, and only 18% of women detected men's flirting.
  - Study 2: outside observers watched 1-minute clips. Flirting was again detected better in clips without flirting. Women's flirting was judged more accurately than men's.
  - The authors explain the pattern by base rates: people assume flirting is rare, so they default to "not flirting."
  - Discrepancy: Psychology Today reports an overall 28% detection figure.
  - — [KU ScholarWorks record](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/65a73d2d-0834-497c-a26e-5aadf7cf0b4a); [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2014/06/03/flirting-hard-detect-study-finds); [Psychology Today (secondary)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/ca/blog/the-psychology-relationships/202010/the-science-flirting-deciphering-subtle-signals)
- **The brief's framing does not match the paper.** The brief says people are poor at detecting flirting "especially women's." In this paper it is *women detecting men's* flirting that was worst (18%), and in the observer study women's flirting was judged *more* accurately. — [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2014/06/03/flirting-hard-detect-study-finds); [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/65a73d2d-0834-497c-a26e-5aadf7cf0b4a)
- **Hall & Xing (2015), *Journal of Nonverbal Behavior*, is a separate paper (weak here, details not verified).** It links verbal and nonverbal flirting behaviours, i.e. which behaviours go with flirting. — [KU news 2015](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2015/02/12/just-time-valentines-day-research-defines-more-behaviors-reveal-romantic-attraction)
- **State effects (moderate, Birnbaum et al.).** After exposure to a sexual stimulus, participants showed more immediacy behaviours towards a potential partner and saw that partner as more attractive and more interested in them. Mood or arousal therefore shifts how interest is read. — [Neuroscience News (secondary)](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-mindset-17072/amp/)

### Inferences
- The sex difference is robust in direction, but most of the evidence is self-report about past misreadings or short lab interactions. "Men overperceive" describes an average, and it is concentrated in men with short-term mating orientation or high self-rated attractiveness.
- Put together, Hall et al. and Perilloux et al. say that both sexes default to "no interest" when flirting is real, especially women reading men. Men add an upward bias when the woman is attractive or they themselves are oriented to short-term mating. These two findings are not contradictory: one measures the *amount* of interest perceived, the other measures *detection* of a binary "flirting or not."

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis of the sexual overperception effect.
- Exact effect sizes for Perilloux et al. (2012) could not be checked because the PDF host was unreachable.
- The Spanish, Chilean and French replication mentioned in search results was not retrieved.

## 2. Ambiguity and plausible deniability as a feature of flirting

### Takeaway
Indirect speech theory (Pinker, Nowak & Lee 2008; Lee & Pinker 2010) treats veiled sexual come-ons as a strategic way to keep plausible deniability and protect the relationship if the other person is not interested. The theory is well cited, but evidence specific to flirting is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **Pinker, Nowak & Lee (2008), *PNAS* 105(3):833–838 (theory backed by a game-theory model and some vignette experiments; weak to moderate for flirting specifically).** The paper names sexual come-ons alongside veiled threats, bribes and polite requests.
  - Indirectness lets a cooperative listener accept while an unwilling listener has no clear basis for an awkward or hostile reaction.
  - Indirectness also avoids the emotional cost of a mismatch in assumed relationship type, e.g. friendship versus sexual. This creates a need for deniability even when nothing tangible is at stake.
  - A direct statement creates "common knowledge" that both people know was said, so it is qualitatively different from a hint even when the intent is clear.
  - — [Harvard PDF](https://scholar.harvard.edu/sites/scholar.harvard.edu/files/pinker/files/pnas_logic_of_indirect_speech.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2242675)
- **Lee & Pinker (2010), *Psychological Review*, the "strategic speaker" account.** It separates off-record indirectness from politeness and has been debated, e.g. by Terkourafi. — [Illinois Experts: "The puzzle of indirect speech"](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/the-puzzle-of-indirect-speech/)
- **The cost of ambiguity shows up in Hall et al.** Because flirting is ambiguous, observers and partners detect it poorly when it happens (see section 1). — [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2014/06/03/flirting-hard-detect-study-finds)

### Inferences
- The same ambiguity that protects the initiator lowers detection rates. That gives a mechanism linking section 1 and section 2: deniability trades accuracy for face-saving. This is an inference, not a tested link.

### Gaps
- I did not find a direct experimental test of plausible deniability in real flirting interactions.

## 3. Escalation, consent, reading disinterest, how rejection is communicated, fear of rejection

### Takeaway
Consent is mostly communicated non-verbally and indirectly, and men lean on non-verbal cues more than women do, which leaves room for miscommunication. Rejecting someone is harder than suitors realise, and people accept unwanted dates to spare the other person's feelings. Safety fears push women towards indirect "soft" rejections and ghosting. Unclear signals can therefore mean disinterest, not encouragement.

### Cited Findings
- **Jozkowski's consent research (moderate, several studies from one lab group).**
  - In a 185-student Indiana University sample, women relied more on verbal consent strategies and men more on non-verbal ones. Men also relied more on non-verbal cues when *interpreting* their partner's consent or non-consent.
  - Jozkowski concluded that men and women hold "disjunctive" views of consent while each believes they understand the other.
  - — [U. Arkansas news](https://news.uark.edu/articles/21460/research-genders-communicate-consent-to-sex-differently); [WIA Report (secondary)](https://www.wiareport.com/?p=12318)
- **Consent to Sex Scale, Jozkowski & Peterson (2014), *Journal of Sex Research* (measure).** 44 items across 5 cue types: non-verbal signals of interest, passive behaviours, verbal cues, initiator behaviours and removal behaviours. — [UCSD EMERGE measure page](https://emerge.ucsd.edu/?p=23208)
- **Rating non-verbal behaviours as consent (weak to moderate, one sample).** Students rated nine non-verbal behaviours and combinations of them as consent signals. Over 43% of women rated *every* behaviour and combination as 0 (not consent), compared with 20.3% of men. Men read more consent into non-verbal behaviour. — [PubMed 32052687](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32052687/)
- **Older baseline, Hall (1998) (weak, secondary citation).** Students reported mostly non-verbal consent. Verbal consent became more frequent as the sexual activity became more intimate. — [U. Missouri document citing it](https://digital.library.missouri.edu/sites/default/files/2025-01/mu_428164.pdf)
- **Bohns & DeVincent (2019), *Social Psychological and Personality Science* 10:1102–1110 (moderate, two preregistered studies).**
  - Studies: a survey of STEM graduate students (N = 942) who recalled unwanted romantic pursuit, and a role-play vignette experiment (N = 385).
  - Suitors underestimated how uncomfortable targets felt saying no and missed how much targets changed their behaviour, including at work, to avoid the suitor.
  - The authors argue this gap can lead suitors to read reluctance as interest, sustaining a cycle of pursuit and evasion.
  - Women were more than twice as likely as men to have been the target.
  - — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/xml/10.1177/1948550618769880); [Cornell Chronicle](https://www.news.cornell.edu/stories/2018/05/research-explores-blind-spots-workplace-suitors)
- **Joel, Teper & MacDonald (2014), *Psychological Science* 25:2233–2240 (moderate, two studies, one lab).** People were more willing to reject an unsuitable date in hypothetical scenarios than when they believed a real person's feelings were at stake. Concern for the suitor's feelings led people to accept advances they would otherwise decline. This is the "people accept dates they don't want" finding. The brief attributes it to Freedman, but the original is Joel et al. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614552828); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/why-people-go-on-dates-they-know-wont-work-out-2); [author PDF mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/2014-joel.pdf)
- **Freedman, Hales, Powell, Le & Williams (2022), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 102:104368 (moderate, two vignette studies, N = 653 in total).**
  - When a safety motive was assigned (e.g., "worried they might lash out physically"), participants were more likely to choose ghosting over explicit rejection.
  - In Study 1 the safety effect appeared when the person being rejected was a man.
  - Results differed between the two studies, and the sample was largely heterosexual.
  - Freedman described forum posts in which men complained about being ghosted and women replied that explicit rejections had drawn aggressive responses. This is anecdotal.
  - — [Ole Miss lab page](https://sites.olemiss.edu/scilab/publications/role-gender-and-safety-concerns-romantic-rejection-decisions); [PsyPost (secondary)](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-have-found-evidence-that-safety-concerns-are-an-important-predictor-of-ghosting/); [Stylist (secondary)](https://www.stylist.co.uk/relationships/dating-love/ghosting-for-safety-rejection-violence/703764)
- **Moran & Burch (2023) (weak, secondary report).** Women worried more than men about what would happen after rejecting an advance, such as being hit or yelled at. — [PsyPost (secondary)](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-have-found-evidence-that-safety-concerns-are-an-important-predictor-of-ghosting/)
- **Fear of rejection.**
  - **Certainty and initiation (moderate).** Harry Reis, a co-author with Birnbaum, says initiating contact is easier when people expect the attraction is shared, because fear of rejection is lower. Birnbaum's 2018 six-study set found that certainty a partner is interested *increases* their sexual appeal. — [Cornell ILR / Rochester coverage](https://archive.ilr.cornell.edu/news/about-ilr/romantic-rejection-research); [News-Medical (secondary)](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20180608/Uncertainty-about-potential-partners-romantic-interest-reduces-sexual-appeal-shows-study.aspx)
  - **Rejection sensitivity (moderate, longitudinal).** Hafen et al. (2014): higher rejection sensitivity at age 16 predicted being less likely to have a romantic partner at age 22. — [UVA teen research](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/teenresearch/publications/accept-or-reject-impact-adolescent-rejection-sensitivity-early-adult)

### Inferences
- Several lines of evidence point the same way: hesitant, polite, delayed or "soft" responses are often disguised rejections, not invitations. Joel et al. show people accept dates they don't want. Bohns & DeVincent show targets accommodate rather than refuse. Freedman et al. show indirect exits are chosen for safety.
- Practical implication: when signals are unclear, slow down or ask directly rather than escalate. This advice follows from the evidence but has not itself been tested.

### Gaps
- The "Freedman et al. 2019" paper named in the brief could not be confirmed. Freedman, Powell, Le & Williams (2019) is a ghosting/"destiny beliefs" paper, which I did not verify. The "accepting unwanted dates" finding belongs to Joel et al. (2014).
- Exact acceptance percentages from Joel et al. could not be checked because spsp.org was unreachable, so none are given.
- I found no direct study quantifying how much initiators *overestimate* the chance of romantic rejection. The liking-gap literature (Boothby et al.) concerns non-romantic conversation and was not retrieved.
- Muehlenhard's consent work was not retrieved in this pass.

## 4. Cultural and gender variation in flirting norms (who initiates, directness)

### Takeaway
Cross-cultural evidence on flirting itself is sparse and mostly compares Western countries. The misperception sex difference holds in the US, Norway and Japan. Which non-verbal cues are used differs by culture even when the goals are the same. Men still initiate most online contact, and women who initiate do well.

### Cited Findings
- **Misperception holds across levels of gender equality (strong for that pattern).** The pattern appears in the US, Norway (Bendixen 2014, N = 308) and Japan (Hiraishi et al. 2016). National gender inequality did not change the reports. — [NTNU record](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2627053?show=full); [LEBS Japan](https://lebs.hbesj.org/index.php/lebs/article/view/lebs.2016.47)
- **Romaniuk (2021/2022), US versus Ukraine (weak to moderate, one study).** Non-verbal behaviour of 170 American and 173 Ukrainian women was coded. Their aims were similar (attraction, interest, seduction), but the non-verbal cues they chose differed by culture. — [Annals of University of Craiova](https://anale-lingvistica.reviste.ucv.ro/index.php/laucv/article/view/18)
- **Kennair et al. (2022), Norway versus US, *Evolutionary Psychology* (moderate).** Sexual-availability cues were rated most effective when used by women in short-term contexts. Friendly contact such as a hug or a kiss on the cheek was not rated as effective. Both samples were Western. — [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/ff38e63f684e4b2589f75c5a6c1d63d8); [PsyPost (secondary)](https://www.psypost.org/2022/05/new-research-sheds-light-on-the-perceived-effectiveness-of-flirtation-tactics-63081)
- **US versus El Salvador conference abstract (2024) (weak, preliminary).** Religiosity was linked to different flirting-style preferences. The authors note there is "little or no evidence" on cross-cultural flirting styles. — [NEIU SRCAS 2024](https://neiudc.neiu.edu/srcas/2024/all/92)
- **Who initiates online (company data, weak to moderate).** OkCupid data show women rarely send the first message, but straight women who do are about 2.5 times more likely to get a reply and tend to talk with more attractive men. — [TIME](https://time.com/4253826/women-okcupid-dating-apps/); [Global Dating Insights](https://www.globaldatinginsights.com/news/okcupid-study-finds-that-women-still-do-not-send-the-first-message/)
- **Anecdote, not data.** A claim that Dutch women approach men directly comes from a single expat account. — [Expatica](https://expatica.com/living/love/how-to-flirt-in-europe-101789)

### Inferences
- Claims that "culture X flirts directly and culture Y flirts indirectly" mostly rest on anecdote. The researchers themselves say systematic cross-cultural flirting data are lacking.

### Gaps
- I found no large multi-country study of who initiates or how direct flirting is. It may exist in courtship-initiation or speed-dating literatures that were not searched here.
- Non-Western and LGBTQ+ flirting norms are largely absent from the sources found.

## 5. Myths: negging/PUA, mirroring, "treat them mean", body-language tells, "women always give clear signals"

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed experiment shows that negging works. The studies found show it is perceived as harmful, and receptivity to pickup-artist techniques goes with sexist attitudes. Mimicry evidence in dating is thin, and some of it comes from an author with multiple retractions. "Hard to get" findings are mixed. "Women always give clear signals" is contradicted by the detection data.

### Cited Findings
- **Negging (myth or unsupported; no efficacy evidence found).**
  - Green, Kukan & Tully (published around 2017 in the *Journal of Aggression, Conflict and Peace Research*): an online vignette survey of N = 308. All forms of negging were rated significantly more harmful than control pickup lines. "Evolved" negging, as taught by dating companies, was seen as more likely to escalate.
  - This study measured perceptions, not outcomes.
  - — [Nottingham repository](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/output/970619/public-perceptions-of-negging-lowering-womens-self-esteem-to-increase-the-male-attractiveness-and-achieve-sexual-conquest); [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/jacpr-06-2016-0235/full/html)
- **Receptivity to PUA tactics (weak to moderate, secondary report).** Hall & Canterberry (2011) surveyed 363 college students and 850 adults. Women with more hostile or benevolent sexist attitudes were more receptive to PUA techniques, and the authors warned about pairing these men and women. — [Jezebel (secondary)](https://jezebel.com/sexist-women-like-pickup-artists-5833986)
- **Dark Triad appeal (mixed).**
  - Carter et al. (2014): N = 128 women rated a high-Dark-Triad character as more attractive.
  - Critics say the effect may reflect extraversion or low neuroticism.
  - A Finnish study found women can detect Dark Triad facial cues, with effects depending on sociosexuality.
  - — [Scott Barry Kaufman summary](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/study-alert-the-dark-triad-personality-attractiveness-to-women); [U. Turku](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/1671488?lang=en_GB)
- **"Mirroring makes them attracted" (weak, with a credibility problem).**
  - Guéguen (2009, *Social Influence*): in speed dating at bars, men mimicked by a woman were more willing to give their contact details.
  - Guéguen has had several papers retracted or given expressions of concern after Brown & Heathers flagged implausible data. Five papers on attractiveness and sexual behaviour were retracted in 2025, and *Social Influence* announced expressions of concern for four papers. I did not confirm whether the mimicry paper is among them.
  - Farley (2014) found in a lab study that mimicry of an attractive confederate went *with* romantic interest. That shows mimicry may signal attraction, not that it causes it.
  - — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/mimicry-improves-womens-speed-dating-success); [Retraction Watch 2025](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/03/03/nicolas-gueguen-five-studies-bust-size-hitchhiking-retracted/); [Retraction Watch 2024](https://retractionwatch.com/2024/04/01/exclusive-bust-size-and-hitchhiking-author-to-earn-four-more-expressions-of-concern/); [Farley, MDSOAR](https://mdsoar.org/handle/11603/4029)
- **"Treat them mean / play hard to get" (mixed).**
  - Birnbaum et al. (2018, *Computers in Human Behavior*, six studies): certainty that a partner is interested *increased* that partner's sexual appeal, which goes against the uncertainty tactic.
  - Birnbaum et al. (2020, *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*): making the chase harder raised desirability.
  - Reis warns that hard-to-get "will backfire" if it reads as disinterest or arrogance.
  - — [News-Medical 2018](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20180608/Uncertainty-about-potential-partners-romantic-interest-reduces-sexual-appeal-shows-study.aspx); [ScienceDaily 2020](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200608192510.htm); [Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/certainty-sexual-attraction-1777322/)
- **"Body-language tells" lists (weak; no validated universal checklist found).** Cue use differs by culture (Romaniuk). Observers detected flirting from video clips poorly (Hall et al. Study 2). Hall & Xing (2015) found some behaviours associated with flirting, but that is association, not a reliable diagnostic. — [Annals of UCV](https://anale-lingvistica.reviste.ucv.ro/index.php/laucv/article/view/18); [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/65a73d2d-0834-497c-a26e-5aadf7cf0b4a); [KU news 2015](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2015/02/12/just-time-valentines-day-research-defines-more-behaviors-reveal-romantic-attraction)
- **"Women always give clear signals" (myth).**
  - Men detected women's flirting only about 36% of the time (Hall et al.).
  - Women often use indirect or ghosting rejections for safety (Freedman et al. 2022).
  - Targets accommodate rather than refuse (Bohns & DeVincent 2019).
  - — [KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2014/06/03/flirting-hard-detect-study-finds); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/psychologists-have-found-evidence-that-safety-concerns-are-an-important-predictor-of-ghosting/); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/xml/10.1177/1948550618769880)

### Inferences
- The burden of proof for PUA claims has not been met. The absence of evidence that negging works, together with evidence that it is seen as harmful, supports labelling it a myth or unsupported. Strictly, "tested and not supported" applies only to perception outcomes.

### Gaps
- The Hales and Gauthier PUA studies named in the brief were not found under those names. No 2023–2026 experiment testing PUA effectiveness was found.
- The ethnographic work on the seduction community was not retrieved.

## 6. Online/app flirting: first messages (brief)

### Takeaway
Reply rates are low. People message "aspirationally," aiming about 25% above their own desirability. Longer messages mostly don't help. Generic openers do worse, according to company data reported second-hand.

### Cited Findings
- **Bruch & Newman (2018), *Science Advances* (moderate, large dataset from an unnamed site in four US cities: New York, Chicago, Seattle, Boston).**
  - Both men and women contacted people about 25% more desirable than themselves.
  - Reply probability dropped sharply as the desirability gap grew. Women's average reply rate was below 20%.
  - Longer messages to more desirable people generally did not raise reply rates, except for Seattle men.
  - More positively worded messages from men got slightly fewer replies.
  - — [MedicalXpress](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2018-08-game-online-dating-men-women.html); [Earth.com](https://www.earth.com/news/online-daters-matches-league)
- **OkCupid company data.** About 32% of first messages got any reply (2009 OkTrends). The claim that "hey" or "hi" lowers reply rates by about 10 percentage points is second-hand from a commercial blog and is not verified. — [OkTrends message-length post mirror](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/okcupid/onlinedatingadviceoptimummessagelength.html); [SwipeStats (secondary, low reliability)](https://www.swipestats.io/blog/pick-up-lines-for-dating-apps)
- **Women initiating does well.** On OkCupid, women who send the first message are about 2.5 times more likely to get a reply. This is company data. — [TIME](https://time.com/4253826/women-okcupid-dating-apps/)

### Inferences
- The online data match the offline findings: flirting is low-yield, and initiators misjudge their odds by aiming high. "Rejection" online is usually silence, which fits the ghosting findings.

### Gaps
- No Hinge first-message data was retrieved.
- Most app data are company blog posts from before 2018. No 2024–2026 peer-reviewed first-message study was found.
