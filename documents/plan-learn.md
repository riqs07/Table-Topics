# Plan: Learn, the educational part of VoiceLab

Proposed 2026-10-18; the user chose its own tab, one screen at a time with swipes, and recall by tapping and by voice. Stages 1 to 3 built the same day (decision 45). Learn teaches what research actually says about speaking, charisma, body language and conversation, in cards you can read in about a minute, and links each idea to practice. The research is in [research/charisma-conversation-duolingo-2026-10.md](research/charisma-conversation-duolingo-2026-10.md); the wider plan is [plan-daily-mix-and-connection.md](plan-daily-mix-and-connection.md).

## Principles

1. **Honest.** Every card carries an evidence label and a source. Myths are named as myths; mixed findings are called mixed. No invented numbers, no hype.
2. **Short.** Three to five screens, about a minute. One idea per card.
3. **Useful.** Every card ends with what to do, and a link to practise it (a drill, a lesson, or a mission).
4. **Remembered.** Each card ends with one recall question, which comes back later (recalling beats rereading; spacing beats cramming).
5. **Kind.** The cards reduce worry ("people notice your nerves far less than you think"), never add it.

## Anatomy of a card

| Screen | What it holds | Example ("Do people notice your nerves?") |
|--------|---------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **1. The question** | A question or a common belief, set like a headline | "Everyone can see how nervous I am." |
| **2. What the research found** | One study or review in two or three plain sentences | "Speakers rated their own nervousness much higher than their audiences did. When speakers were told about this, they felt calmer and their speeches were rated better." |
| **3. What it means** | Why it matters, in one or two lines | "Your racing heart is mostly invisible. The audience sees far less than you feel." |
| **4. What to do** | One or two practical lines, then "Try it" | "Before you start, remind yourself: they can't see most of this. Then pause and say your first line." **Try it:** an opening drill |
| **5. Remember it** | One recall question (tap an answer), then the evidence label and source | "Compared with how nervous you feel, how nervous do you look? Much less / About the same / More." *Evidence: moderate (one study). Source: Savitsky & Gilovich 2003.* |

**Card types:**
- **Explainer:** the anatomy above.
- **Myth or fact:** screen 1 is a claim; you guess "myth" or "fact" before seeing the research. The guess is itself retrieval practice, and surprising answers stick.
- **Try it now:** a short explainer ending in a 20-second voice exercise in the card itself, such as saying a sentence with more pitch variety, or turning a closed question into an open one.

**Evidence labels:**
- **Strong:** replicated, or a large review.
- **Moderate:** a good study or two.
- **Mixed:** studies disagree.
- **Myth:** popular, but not supported.

Each label is shown in words, never as a colour alone.

## Recall and review

- Each card's recall question is answered by tapping.
- It comes back after about a day, a week, then a month, as a single question in the daily mix's Connection lane, or as a "quick review" line on Today. A wrong answer brings it back sooner.
- No hearts or penalties: a wrong answer just shows the card's key line again.
- Progress per set: cards read, and recall questions answered correctly the last time they came round.

## The catalogue (first version, about 35 cards)

### Set 1: Nerves and presence (speaking)

| Card | Type | Key point | What to do / Try it | Evidence |
|------|------|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| Do people notice your nerves? | Explainer | Speakers think their nerves show far more than they do | Remind yourself before you start; an opening drill | Moderate (Savitsky & Gilovich 2003) |
| Nobody is watching that closely | Explainer | The spotlight effect: we overestimate how much others notice us, roughly double | Let a stumble go and carry on; a Table Topic | Strong (Gilovich et al. 2000) |
| "I'm excited" vs "I'm calm" | Myth or fact | Saying "I'm excited" may change how you feel; a 2025 replication found no change in how speeches were rated | Try it if it helps you; don't expect miracles | Mixed (Brooks 2014; Poynter & Pasqualini 2025) |
| Are "um" and "uh" mistakes? | Myth or fact | They are words that signal a short delay; listeners even use "uh". A few are normal, many distract | Swap some for a silent pause; a pause drill | Moderate (Clark & Fox Tree 2002) |
| Silence feels longer to you | Explainer | A pause that feels long to you sounds natural to listeners and gives your point room | Pause before your key line; lesson "Pause before the point" | Practical guidance (labelled as such) |
| Power posing | Myth or fact | Standing like a superhero did not change hormones or behaviour in a larger study; you may feel a bit more powerful | Stand however you're comfortable; use the time to breathe | Myth for the hormone claim (Ranehill et al. 2015) |

### Set 2: Charisma

| Card | Type | Key point | What to do / Try it | Evidence |
|------|------|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| What charisma is | Explainer | Two parts: influence (presence, leading) and warmth (making people comfortable) | Notice which one you lean on | Moderate (Tskhay et al. 2018) |
| Can charisma be learned? | Myth or fact | Fact: managers trained in specific behaviours and given feedback were rated more charismatic | Practise one tactic this week; a prepared speech | Moderate (Antonakis et al. 2011) |
| Stories, contrasts and threes | Try it now | Charismatic speakers use stories, contrasts ("not X, but Y") and lists of three | Say one contrast about your week | Moderate (Antonakis et al. 2011) |
| Your voice carries charisma | Try it now | More pitch variety and a lively pace are heard as more charismatic | Say one sentence flat, then with varied pitch; listen back | Moderate (Niebuhr and colleagues) |
| A real smile | Explainer | Smiles that reach the eyes are seen as more genuine and trustworthy, though people can fake them | Smile when something is actually funny or nice, not as a rule | Moderate (Gunnery & Ruben 2016) |
| Do stories always win? | Myth or fact | Stories help people stay with you, but one big experiment found them no more persuasive than facts | Use a story to hold attention, then make your point plainly | Mixed |

### Set 3: Body language, myth or fact

| Card | Type | Key point | What to do | Evidence |
|------|------|-----------|------------|----------|
| "93% of communication is non-verbal" | Myth or fact | Myth: from narrow lab studies on liking; the researcher himself says it does not apply to communication in general | Your words matter; so does how you say them | Myth (Mehrabian's own clarification) |
| Can you read emotions from faces? | Myth or fact | Not reliably: the same expression can mean different things; context matters | Treat a face as a question, and ask | Strong review (Barrett et al. 2019) |
| Can you spot a liar? | Myth or fact | Barely: people are right 54% of the time, experts no better | Don't play detective; listen instead | Strong (Bond & DePaulo 2006) |
| Crossed arms mean defensive | Myth or fact | Not reliable on its own: people cross arms when cold or comfortable | Notice changes in a person, not single poses | Follows from the reviews above |
| Mirroring people | Myth or fact | Mixed: some mimicry goes with liking, but deliberate copying can backfire if noticed | Don't copy; pay attention and it happens naturally | Mixed (replications) |
| Eye contact | Explainer | Mutual eye contact comes at moments of shared attention and then breaks; staring is not the goal | Look at them while they talk; look away naturally while you think | Strong single study (Wohltjen & Wheatley 2021) |
| Notice changes, not signals | Explainer | Watch for changes: someone lighting up, answers getting shorter, glancing away more. Then adjust, or ask | A Connection mission: notice what made someone light up | Synthesis of the above |
| First impressions | Explainer | Seconds of behaviour shape impressions, but snap judgments are where stereotypes do harm | Give people a second impression | Moderate (Ambady & Rosenthal) |

### Set 4: Conversation

| Card | Type | Key point | What to do / Try it | Evidence |
|------|------|-----------|---------------------|----------|
| People like you more than you think | Explainer | After talking, people underestimate how much the other person liked them | Assume it went better than it felt | Strong (Boothby et al. 2018) |
| The power of follow-up questions | Explainer | Asking more follow-up questions makes people like you more | Ask one follow-up today; a follow-up drill | Strong (Huang et al. 2017) |

| Boomerasking | Myth or fact | Asking a question just to answer it yourself makes you seem less sincere | Ask questions you can't answer yourself | Strong (Brooks & Yeomans 2025) |
| Listeners shape the story | Explainer | When listeners react specifically ("no way!"), storytellers tell their stories better | React to the story, not just "mm-hmm"; a listening drill | Strong (Bavelas et al. 2000) |
| Support, don't shift | Explainer | "Oh, I just ate" shifts to you; "When did you last eat?" keeps it on them | Count your shifts in one conversation | Observational (Derber) |
| Checking you understood | Myth or fact | Paraphrasing is not a magic formula: it helps when it checks understanding, not as a technique | Say back the feeling, not just the facts | Mixed (Weger et al.) |

| Conversations run long | Explainer | Conversations rarely end when both want them to, so a warm exit is a kindness | End one conversation on a high point | Strong (Mastroianni et al. 2021) |
| Remembering names | Try it now | Link a name to something meaningful, use it once, and recall it later | Say a name, its link, then recall it after a minute | Moderate |

## Where Learn appears

- **Its own place:** the Learn tab (or a Learn section inside Skills, depending on the tab decision), with the six sets as paths and done marks, like the lessons.
- **Inside practice:** a card's key line shows where it applies. For example, "People like you more than you think" appears on a Connection mission's debrief, and "Do people notice your nerves?" on a Table Topic's start screen, as a small "Why?" link.
- **In the daily mix:** a new card or a recall question can be the Connection step on lighter days.
- **After feedback:** when the notes point at a skill (many fillers, a boomerask), they link to the card that explains it.

## Writing rules for cards

- Plain words; speak to "you". No jargon unless explained in the same line.
- Name the finding, then the source in short form; full citations live in the research note.
- Say how strong the evidence is. When a finding changes, update the card and its label.
- Never claim the app can read emotions, detect lies, or judge personality.
- Keep body-language advice about noticing and adjusting, not decoding.
