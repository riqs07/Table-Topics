# Teach warmth by doing, not by choosing

Charisma in everyday life is two things: **influence** (presence, leading) and **affability** (putting people at ease). Most of what makes people like and trust you in conversation sits on the affability side, and it is made of small, teachable acts. You ask about their answer. You react with real interest to their good news. You match how much they share, then hand the turn back. You listen without fixing. You leave while it is still good. The evidence for these acts ranges from strong (disclosure and liking, follow-up questions) to moderate (good-news replies, compliments, vulnerability), and several popular tips are weak or myths (copying body language, using someone's name, "small talk makes you unhappy"). On training, the clearest lesson is that **practice moves behaviour and reading does not**: rehearsal with specific feedback, plus real-world exposure, is what works. The one randomised test of an SJT-style programme found **no change in behaviour**, so text scenario questions should be the recognition step in a chain (learn, choose, say it aloud, try it for real), never the whole lesson. For VoiceLab this means about ten new Learn cards, a predict-do-compare debrief on every mission, a new "responding to news" unit, and a scenario question type that always ends with the learner saying the move aloud in their own words.

This report builds on the earlier note, [charisma-conversation-duolingo-2026-10.md](charisma-conversation-duolingo-2026-10.md). That note already covers body-language myths, the liking gap, boomerasking, co-narrating listeners, nerves, and what to borrow from Duolingo. Those points are only referred to here, not repeated.

**Evidence labels used throughout:** **strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **moderate** (several studies, often from one lab, or one good trial), **mixed** (replications disagree), **weak** (one small study, a secondary report, or expert opinion), **myth** (tested and not supported, or no support found for a popular claim).

## Warmth is the half of charisma conversation can move

People judge others on two axes: warmth (are you friendly and trustworthy?) and competence (can you act on it?). Fiske, Cuddy and Glick call these "universal dimensions of social cognition" and argue that warmth is judged first, because we must decide friend or foe before anything else ([Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/universal-dimensions-of-social-cognition-warmth-and-competence/); [Cuddy et al. 2008](https://culturalq.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Cuddy-Paper.pdf)). The two-axis structure is **strong**. "Warmth first" is **moderate**.

Charisma research maps onto the same split. Tskhay and colleagues' six-item General Charisma Inventory found two factors: influence and affability. Higher scorers were more persuasive ([GCI validation summary](https://cipp.ug.edu.pl/Assessment-of-the-factorial-and-criterion-validity-of-the-General-Charisma-Inventory,131996,0,2.html); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com:443/us/blog/the-attraction-doctor/202312/the-science-of-charisma-for-romance-work-and-everyday-life)). **Moderate**. The app already has this as a card.

The useful step is to sort VoiceLab's two halves this way. The speech side (voice, stories, structure, Antonakis-style tactics) trains **influence**. The Connect side trains **affability**. Every behaviour in the next section reads as a warmth signal. A practical rule follows: in a first meeting, show interest before you show expertise.

### Responsiveness is the engine underneath

Modern relationship science explains why these warmth acts work. **Perceived partner responsiveness** is the feeling that someone **understands** you, **validates** you and **cares** about you ([Maisel, Gable & Strachman 2008](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/gable/shelly/sites/labs.psych.ucsb.edu.gable.shelly/files/pubs/maisel_et_al._2008.pdf)). It predicts intimacy and satisfaction in existing relationships (**strong**) ([Visserman et al., JPSP](https://labsites.rochester.edu/lelab/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Visserman-et-al.-in-press.-Lightening-the-Load-Perceived-Partner-Responsiveness-Fosters-More-Positive-Appraisals-of-Relational-Sacrifices.-JPSP.pdf)). Among strangers, more interaction led to more attraction, partly through responsiveness (**moderate**, single studies) ([Reis et al. 2011](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/InPress-a_ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_JPSP.pdf)).

Two points make this useful for an app. First, responsiveness is "clearly influenced by the actual behavior of the responder", so it can be trained ([Maisel et al. 2008](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/gable/shelly/sites/labs.psych.ucsb.edu.gable.shelly/files/pubs/maisel_et_al._2008.pdf)). Second, its three parts make a simple scoring frame for any reply. Did it show you **understood** (a specific detail or accurate check)? Did it **validate** (their reaction makes sense)? Did it show **care** (interest in what happens next for them)? This frame can drive the coach's notes, the live partner's rubric and the scenario feedback.

## The acts that build connection, with honest labels

The table below is the core of the findings. Items marked "in app" are already covered by an existing Learn card or Connect unit; the label is confirmed or updated here.

| Behaviour | Label | What the research found | Do this |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follow-up questions (in app) | **strong–moderate** | Speed daters who asked more follow-ups got more second dates; people don't predict this ([Huang et al. 2017](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/35647952)) | Ask about their answer before changing topic |
| Disclosure and liking | **strong** | Meta-analysis: we like people who disclose to us, disclose to those we like, and like those we disclosed to. Inappropriate disclosure lowers liking ([Collins & Miller 1994](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7809308/)) | Share, don't only ask |
| Back-and-forth, gradually deeper sharing | **moderate** | Pairs who took turns disclosing liked each other more than pairs who disclosed one after the other ([Sprecher et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033)); the 36 questions raised felt closeness in the lab, not commitment ([GGIA](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness); [Anglia Ruskin](https://aru.ac.uk/news/can-36-questions-really-change-your-love-life)) | Match their depth, add one notch, hand back |
| Precise "too early" disclosure rules | **weak** | No experiment isolates timing. The bigger risk is one-sided sharing (expert opinion) ([CP report](https://www.castanetkamloops.net/news/Business/611187/Oversharing-can-build-trust-and-strengthen-relationships-experts-say-but-context-matters)) | Avoid long heavy monologues; keep it two-way |
| Active-constructive reply to good news | **moderate** | Enthusiastic, curious replies go with more trust and closeness; "nice" and downside-pointing do not ([Gable & Reis 2010](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/GableReis_2010.pdf); [Reis lab](https://sas.rochester.edu/psy/sites/reis/research/)). Mostly correlational; ideal intensity varies by culture ([Sim et al.](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/50175/)) | Real reaction, one or two specific questions, no hijack |
| High-quality listening (in app) | **strong** (correlational), **moderate** (experiments) | Perceived listening and relationship quality: r = .51 across a registered meta-analysis, though 75% of studies were self-report ([Kluger et al. 2024](https://www.hermanaguinis.com/pdf/JBPlistening.pdf)). Good listening lowered speakers' anxiety and defensiveness ([Itzchakov et al. 2017](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/i-am-aware-of-my-inconsistencies-but-can-tolerate-them-the-effect/)) | Full attention, specific reactions |
| Advice vs listening | **moderate** | Active listening made people feel more understood than advice did, but both raised liking only a little ([Weger et al. 2014](https://reachfamilies.umn.edu/sites/default/files/prtw/2021-09/Weger_2014__2021091010606.pdf)) | Hold advice until asked |
| Paraphrasing for liking (in app) | **mixed** | Raised the listener's attractiveness, not feeling understood ([Weger et al. 2010](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/1477)) | Check understanding; no script |
| Fast, smooth replies | **moderate** | Conversations with faster replies felt more connected; the gaps (<250 ms) are too short to control consciously ([Templeton et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794835)) | Listen fully; speed follows attention |
| Deep over shallow talk (in app) | **moderate–strong** | 12 experiments: deep talk with strangers went better than people expected ([Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk)) | Go one question deeper |
| "Small talk makes you unhappy" | **myth** | The original link (n = 79) did not replicate in a pooled N = 486; substantive talk still went with life satisfaction ([Milek et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6139582/)) | Small talk is a fine on-ramp |
| Compliments | **moderate** | Givers underestimated how good recipients would feel and overestimated awkwardness ([Boothby & Bohns 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167220949003)) | Give the specific compliment you'd hold back |
| Gratitude | **moderate** | Thanked mentors rated the thanker warmer and were more likely to share contact details ([UNSW](https://www.unsw.edu.au/newsroom/news/2014/09/more-than-words--saying-thank-you-does-make-a-difference)). One study, n = 70 | Thank with a specific reason |
| Showing vulnerability | **moderate** | People judge others' admissions more kindly than their own ([Big Think on Bruk et al. 2018](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/the-beautiful-mess-effect-other-people-view-our-vulnerability-more-positively-than-we-do)). One lab, scenario-based, may cost status for bosses ([ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/g-beautiful-mess-effect-vulnerability-status/)) | Admit small mistakes; ask for help |
| Leaving earlier (in app) | **moderate** | Conversations almost never ended when both wanted ([ZME Science on Mastroianni et al. 2021](https://zmescience.com/science/most-conversations-dont-end-when-people-want-them-to-study-finds)) | Leave on a high point |
| Humor | **weak** for likability | Mostly mate-choice research; self-deprecation helped only high-status speakers ([Greengross & Miller 2008](https://doaj.org/article/dcf0172c3a6e416881b935bbfcb44439)) | Light and inclusive; don't make "be funny" a goal |
| Deliberate mimicry (in app) | **mixed** | A preregistered study found no effect of mimicry on rapport or trust ([Hale & Hamilton 2016](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1531017/1/srep35295.pdf)); another replication was inconclusive ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-affiliative-consequences-of-same-race-and-cross-race-mimicry/)) | Pay attention; don't copy |
| Using their name to be liked (in app) | **myth / unsupported** | No peer-reviewed evidence found that saying someone's name raises liking | Remember names; don't sprinkle them |

Three patterns run through this table. First, most warm acts are **undervalued**. People underestimate how much a compliment lands, how well deep talk goes, how kindly their vulnerability is seen, and how much their partner liked them. Kardas and colleagues showed that correcting these expectations made people choose deeper questions ([Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/deep-conversations-strangers-are-more-fulfilling-small-talk)). So a big part of VoiceLab's job is to **lower the felt cost** of warm acts, and "your fear is miscalibrated" is a strong card format.

Second, **attention beats technique** for nonverbal rapport. Tickle-Degnen and Rosenthal's model puts attentiveness and positivity first in early meetings, with coordination coming later ([Tickle-Degnen & Rosenthal 1990](https://www.nationalcac.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/The-nature-of-rapport-and-its-nonverbal-correlates.pdf)). The mimicry and turn-timing results fit this. Copying on purpose does little. Fast replies come from listening, not from rushing.

Third, the **balance between asking and sharing** matters. Questions build liking, but disclosure is half the engine of closeness. "Never talk about yourself" is wrong. The target is a two-way rhythm, not an interview.

One correction for the app: Connect Unit 5's mission "use someone's name twice" rests on unsupported advice. Change it to "remember one new name and use it when you say goodbye", framed as a memory habit, not a liking trick.

## Practice with feedback and real exposure is what changes behaviour

Training evidence is clearer about *how* to train than about how big the gains are for ordinary adults.

**Social skills training helps, modestly, and mostly through practice.** There is almost no meta-analysis of skills training for general adults. In adult social phobia, skills training did about as well as exposure and cognitive restructuring (mean effect 0.77 after treatment), which points to repeated contact with feared situations as a shared ingredient ([meta-analysis PDF](https://um.es/metaanalysis/pdf/5005.pdf)). In non-clinical youth, an analysis of 839 effects found d = 0.37, with larger effects when programmes included skill-building ([Mooij et al. 2020](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/52487875/Mooij2020_Article_EffectiveComponentsOfSocialSki.pdf)). Doing homework between sessions predicted better outcomes (r = .22) ([Kazantzis et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8687364)). Label: **moderate** in clinical groups, **weak** for general adults.

**Role-play plus feedback beats lectures; rules beat scripts.** An overview of 12 reviews of physician training found role-play, feedback and small-group discussion worked; lectures and written material only supported them ([UCL review citing Berkhof 2011](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10084578/1/Literature%20review%20on%20teaching%20communication%20skills%20to%20healthcare%20workers%20FINAL%203rd%20July%202014.pdf)). Behaviour modelling worked better when learning points were stated as rules behind the behaviour rather than scripts to copy ([QIC-WD summary of Taylor et al. 2005](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Behavior%20Modeling%20Training%20112421b.pdf)). **Moderate**. This is the single most important design rule for scenario items: teach the move, not the line.

**Gains shrink on the way to real life.** Oncology communication training improved clinician behaviour by 0.54 but patient outcomes by only 0.13, a non-significant trend ([Barth & Lannen 2011](https://boris-portal.unibe.ch/entities/publication/21c6274f-8a02-46c4-88d3-89c658d27771)). Skills fade without use, reaching d ≈ −1.4 after a year, and thinking-type skills fade fastest ([Arthur et al. 1998](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/1998-arthur.pdf)). **Moderate–strong**. The implication: measure real-world use, and keep light practice going rather than "graduating" learners.

**Real-world missions work, framed as experiments.** In a week-long app study, 286 students who completed "talk to a stranger" missions became less worried about rejection and more confident, with effects lasting at least a week. 87% of 1,336 conversations were with the first person approached. Students who only *observed* strangers did not change ([Sandstrom, Boothby & Cooney](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Talking_to_strangers_a_week-long_intervention_reduces_psychological_barriers_to_social_connection/23490227); [SPSP](https://spsp.org/news/character-and-context-blog/sandstrom-boothby-cooney-talking-to-strangers)). **Moderate**, one study. In clinical work, dropping safety behaviours (rehearsing lines in your head, over-preparing) added benefit to exposure ([McManus et al. 2008](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:0abb384b-ee45-43ef-97be-6dbf43b8c3c7); [PubMed 10483844](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10483844)), though not all researchers agree ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioural-and-cognitive-psychotherapy/article/exploring-the-impact-of-safety-behaviour-use-on-cognitive-psychophysiological-emotional-and-behavioural-responses-during-a-speech-task/D07258F51395121223C1DAF2C37EA460)). The mechanism is the gap between what you predicted and what happened. That is why missions should log a prediction first.

**Feedback must be about the task, not the person.** Feedback improved performance by d = 0.41 on average, but more than a third of feedback interventions made performance *worse*, especially when feedback drew attention to the self ([Kluger & DeNisi 1996](https://albany.edu/faculty/ts460/pad634/Files/2361-KlugerDeNisiFeedbackcapHighlighted.pdf)). **Strong**. "You asked three questions; try one about their answer" is good. "You seem awkward" is harmful, especially for anxious users.

**AI partners help when they give personal feedback.** In a preregistered trial of 968 people, brief personalised LLM coaching after role-play improved empathic communication more than practice alone or generic video feedback ([Kumar et al. 2026, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.15245)). VR social-skills training beat alternatives on average, but gamified versions did slightly *worse* ([Howard & Gutworth 2020](https://researchwith.montclair.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-virtual-reality-training-programs-for-social-s/)). **Moderate** for in-simulation gains, **weak** for transfer to real conversations.

**Short daily practice is reasonable, though untested directly.** Spaced practice beats massed practice (d = 0.46) ([Donovan & Radosevich 1999](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/1999-donovan.pdf)), and habits took a median of 66 days in one small study ([Surrey](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)). No trial compares 10 minutes a day with longer blocks for social skills. **Weak/indirect**. About two months of daily practice is a defensible plan length.

Put together, the training chain is: **learn the principle → recognise it → produce it aloud → try it for real → compare with your prediction → review it later.** VoiceLab already has the first, third and (soon) fourth links. Scenario questions fill the second. The predict-compare debrief is the missing piece.

## Text scenario questions: useful for recognition, weak on their own

### What the evidence does and does not support

SJT-style items are well-established **measures**. They predict job performance modestly (validities around .20–.26) and are best seen as tests of knowledge about effective behaviour ([Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-validity-of-single-response-situational-judgment-tests-a-nomo/); [Lievens et al. 2008](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5678)). That knowledge framing matters: if items tap general knowledge of what works socially, that knowledge is teachable.

As **training**, the evidence is thin. The only randomised test found, a 2018 cluster-randomised study in medical residency programmes, combined an online SJT, a feedback report, group debriefing and goal-setting. It did **not** improve residents' professionalism or interpersonal performance, although participants liked it ([CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/61068158)). A 2026 trial (N = 269) found that feedback design in an online communication SJT changed motivation and cognitive load, with effects depending on the learner, but did not report behaviour change ([Reiser et al. 2026](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10872981.2026.2639198)). Label: **weak** that scenario items change behaviour. VoiceLab should say so in its "How this works" page.

The case for using them anyway comes from general learning science, which is **strong** but tested mostly on facts, not social judgement:

- **Competitive wrong options teach.** Multiple-choice tests improved recall of the tested material *and* of related material that appeared as plausible wrong options, because learners reason about every option to reject it ([Little & Bjork 2014](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2017/01/LittleBjorkMC2014.pdf)). Silly options teach nothing.
- **Explained feedback beats right/wrong.** Formative feedback should be specific, supportive and non-judging ([Shute 2008](https://myweb.fsu.edu/vshute/pdf/shute%202008_b.pdf)). Picking a wrong option without feedback can strengthen the error ([CDL review](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt9xn3f39q/qt9xn3f39q.pdf?t=op2n6h)).
- **Confident errors get fixed best.** Errors made with high confidence are more likely to be corrected after feedback ([Metcalfe review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3604148)).
- **Guessing first helps.** Trying to answer before seeing options or explanations improves later learning, even when the guess is wrong, and learners don't notice the benefit ([Pan & Rivers 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1); [Pan & Carpenter 2023](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10648-023-09805-6)).
- **Saying it aloud helps memory a little.** The production effect is reliable (g ≈ 0.37), but smaller when everything is said aloud ([Forrin & MacLeod 2016](https://uwaterloo.ca/memory-attention-cognition-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/forrin-macleod-2016-cjep.pdf)). The real reason to add a spoken step is that recognising a good reply is a different skill from producing one in two seconds.
- **Rate-each formats carry more signal.** One emotion-management test had reliability .68 as pick-one and .92 as rate-each-option ([Frontiers 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01156/full)).

Comparable apps confirm both the pattern and the risk. ConversationBuilderTeen has teens pick the best of three lines and then record themselves saying it, but a reviewer noted that on tricky topics "it's not always clear why the correct response is the best response" ([Common Sense Media](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/app-reviews/conversationbuilderteen)). On Cue - Social shows what most people chose and why responses work ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/on-cue/id6758286148)). No adult product found combines research cards, scenario items, spoken drills, a daily plan and missions.

### Format: one item, five steps

1. **Situation.** Two to three lines. A specific person, place and line of dialogue. Context that changes the answer goes in the stem ("your manager, in a two-minute hallway chat").
2. **Warm-up guess (occasional).** On the first item of a unit only: "What would you say?" Type or say it. Not graded. Label it "warm-up".
3. **Choose.** "What's the best thing to say?" (not "what would you say?"). Three or four options. An optional one-tap "Sure / Not sure" before reveal.
4. **Reveal.** Each option shows its tier and the named pattern behind it. The best option gets one line on why. The chosen option, if not best, gets one line on why it falls short, side by side with the best one. One principle line closes: the move, not the wording.
5. **Say it aloud.** "Now say the move in your own words." Record up to 15 seconds. Notes (not a score) check for the move: did you ask about their detail, did you avoid shifting to yourself. On some items, the voice partner replies and the learner gives one more turn.

### Grading: four tiers, not right and wrong

| Tier | Points | Feedback tone |
|---|---|---|
| **Best** | 3 | "Yes. Here's why it works." |
| **Also good** | 2 | "This works too. The best one also…" |
| **Flat** | 1 | "Not wrong, but it gives them little to work with." |
| **Misstep** | 0 | "Common move. Here's what it does to them." |

No hearts, no lost lives. A misstep is the most useful moment in the item, not a penalty.

### Design rules

1. **Teach the move, not the line.** Feedback names the principle and says "in your own words". Rules beat scripts in modelling research.
2. **Every wrong option is a named, tempting mistake.** Use the catalogue: shift to yourself, boomerask, flat acknowledgement, dead-end closed question, topic switch, interrogation stack, unsolicited advice, pointing out the downside, one-upping, over-sharing, retreating to safe talk, seeking reassurance, generic praise, bluffing. Never include an obviously rude option.
3. **Specific, realistic stems.** Write from real incidents. Ask friends about recent awkward moments, as the emotion-management test builders did ([edinstruments](https://edinstruments.org/node/563)).
4. **No giveaways.** The best answer is not always the longest, warmest-sounding or last option. Vary its length and position. Option length is one of the few item-writing rules with test evidence ([Rodriguez 2014](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/download/b80d8639c49c02148efe594c4d30e08479fb6dfdbdc552674e7071d20ca03429/116532/Rodriguez_2014_Distractor_functioning_in_modofied_items_for_test.pdf)).
5. **Key with three raters.** Have at least three people rank each item independently. If they disagree on the best option, the item is an "it depends" item: rewrite the stem to fix the context, or drop it. Experienced raters made slightly better keys ([record](https://karla.hds.hebis.de/EdsRecord/buh%252C187616682)).
6. **Pair items where context flips the answer.** The same news from a close friend and from a manager in a hurry can need different replies. Pairs like this are contrasting cases.
7. **Flag culture.** Set items in one stated frame (casual English-speaking settings). Add "norms vary" on items about enthusiasm, compliments, personal questions or disagreement. Most SJT items travelled from the US to Spain, but not all ([Lievens et al. 2015](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5747)).
8. **Aim for about 70% first-try success.** Duolingo's Birdbrain targets roughly that on new material ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/how-duolingo-uses-ai-in-every-part-of-its-app)).
9. **Re-test the principle, not the item.** Review missed items later with new surface details. Repeated identical items mostly measure memory of the item ([Lievens et al. 2008](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/lkcsb_research/5678)).
10. **Track transfer cheaply.** Log first-try accuracy on unseen items per principle over time. That is the app's own check that the knowledge generalises.

### Eight sample items for VoiceLab

#### 1. The job offer (responding to good news)

*Situation.* At lunch, your coworker Priya puts her phone down, grinning. "I just got the offer. The Lisbon job. I start in March!"

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. "No way, Lisbon! When did you hear? What did you do when you read it?" | **Best** | Active-constructive |
| B. "Congrats! I moved abroad for work once. Honestly the first year was brutal…" | Misstep | Shift to yourself |
| C. "Nice one, well done." | Flat | Passive-constructive |
| D. "Big move. Have you thought about how you'll find a flat?" | Misstep | Pointing out the downside |

*Why A is best.* It shows real enthusiasm and asks her to relive the moment. That is the reply linked to more trust and closeness ([Gable & Reis 2010](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/GableReis_2010.pdf)).
*Why the others are weaker.* B takes the spotlight just as she steps into it. C is kind but ends the moment; she has nothing to say next. D answers joy with a problem; it reads as doubt even when meant as help.
*Note.* Norms vary. In some cultures a quieter warm reply is ideal ([Sim et al.](https://research.sabanciuniv.edu/id/eprint/50175/)). The core is "engaged and specific", not loud.
*Say it aloud.* "React to her news in your own words, and ask one thing about the moment."

#### 2. The half marathon (following up on free information)

*Situation.* At a friend's party, you ask Tom how his weekend was. "Mostly recovering. I ran my first half marathon on Saturday."

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. "Cool. So how do you know Hannah?" | Misstep | Topic switch (missed free information) |
| B. "Your first one! How did the last few miles feel?" | **Best** | Follow-up on their detail |
| C. "Congrats! Was it as hard as you expected?" | Also good | Follow-up, closed-ish |
| D. "What was your time? Did you follow a plan? How long did you train?" | Flat | Interrogation stack |

*Why B is best.* It picks up the word "first" and asks about his experience, not the facts. Follow-up questions are the kind that raised liking ([Huang et al. 2017](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/35647952)).
*Why the others are weaker.* A drops the most interesting thing he said. C works, but it invites a shorter answer than B. D asks three questions at once; it feels like a form, and he can only answer one.
*Say it aloud.* "Ask one follow-up about his experience."

#### 3. Monday coffee (boomerasking)

*Situation.* It's Monday morning at the coffee machine with Dan from another team. You had a great weekend hiking and would like to mention it.

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. "How was your weekend? Mine was amazing, we hiked up…" | Misstep | Boomerask |
| B. "I had the best weekend. Went hiking in the Peaks. How was yours?" | **Best** | Honest share, then hand back |
| C. "How was your weekend?" (then listen) | Also good | Open question |
| D. "Fine, thanks." (when he asks you) | Flat | Dead end |

*Why B is best.* It shares openly, which builds closeness, then truly hands the turn over. Asking a question only to answer it yourself makes you seem less sincere (see the earlier note's boomerasking section).
*Why the others are weaker.* A disguises your story as interest in his; people notice. C is good, but if you also want to share, B is more honest. D gives him nothing to follow up on.
*Say it aloud.* "Share one detail from your weekend, then ask about his."

#### 4. The brutal week (listening before fixing)

*Situation.* Your friend Sam sighs. "Work's been brutal. My manager moved the deadline up again, and I'm the only one on the project."

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. "You should push back. Or honestly, start looking for something else." | Flat | Unsolicited advice |
| B. "Ugh, same. My boss does that constantly." | Misstep | Shift to yourself (one-upping) |
| C. "So you're carrying it alone and the goalposts keep moving. That sounds exhausting. What's the hardest part right now?" | **Best** | Understand, validate, care |
| D. "That sucks." | Flat | Generic acknowledgement |

*Why C is best.* It shows understanding (a specific summary), validation ("sounds exhausting") and care (a question about them). Active listening made people feel more understood than advice did ([Weger et al. 2014](https://reachfamilies.umn.edu/sites/default/files/prtw/2021-09/Weger_2014__2021091010606.pdf)).
*Why the others are weaker.* A may be right, but too soon; Sam hasn't asked. B turns their bad week into yours. D is kind but generic, so Sam has to work to keep going.
*Say it aloud.* "Say back what's hard for Sam, then ask one question."

#### 5. Why they moved (matching depth)

*Situation.* At a dinner, someone you've just met says, "Honestly, I moved here because my dad got sick and I wanted to be closer."

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. "Oh. So what do you do for work?" | Misstep | Retreat to safe talk |
| B. "That's a big thing to do. How is he doing now?" | **Best** | Match depth, stay with them |
| C. "I'm sorry. My mum's been unwell too, so I get some of that. How's he doing?" | Also good | Small matching share, hand back |
| D. "I know how that is. My grandmother was ill for years, and the hospital was…" (two minutes) | Flat | Over-sharing / hijack |

*Why B is best.* They took a step deeper. B meets them there and keeps the focus on them. Back-and-forth matched sharing builds liking ([Sprecher et al. 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407512459033)).
*Why the others are weaker.* A signals that their honesty made you uncomfortable. C is good: a short matching share, then back to them. D starts the same way but turns into your story; one-sided sharing is the bigger risk.
*Say it aloud.* "Acknowledge what they shared and ask one caring question."

#### 6. Shorter answers (ending well)

*Situation.* You've talked with Mei for fifteen minutes at a work event. It went well. Now her answers are getting shorter and she keeps glancing at the bar.

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. Ask another question to keep it going | Misstep | Staying past the peak |
| B. "Anyway, I should go." | Flat | Abrupt exit |
| C. "I've really enjoyed this, especially your bike trip story. I'll let you grab a drink. Good luck with the launch!" | **Best** | Warm exit with a callback |
| D. "Sorry, am I boring you?" | Misstep | Seeking reassurance |

*Why C is best.* It ends on a high point, names a specific detail (showing you listened) and wishes her well. Conversations usually run longer than at least one person wants ([ZME Science on Mastroianni et al.](https://zmescience.com/science/most-conversations-dont-end-when-people-want-them-to-study-finds)).
*Why the others are weaker.* A keeps her in a talk she may want to leave. B is fine but cold after a good chat. D puts her on the spot to reassure you; it turns her tiredness into your problem.
*Say it aloud.* "End the conversation warmly, mentioning one thing she said."

#### 7. After her talk (giving a compliment)

*Situation.* Your colleague Ana just gave a presentation. Someone asked a hard budget question, and she handled it well. You're both at the coffee machine.

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. Say nothing; it might seem awkward | Misstep | Holding back a compliment |
| B. "Good talk." | Flat | Generic praise |
| C. "The way you handled the budget question was great. Calm, and you actually answered it." | **Best** | Specific compliment |
| D. "Great talk! Mine next week will be a disaster compared to that." | Flat | Deflecting to yourself |

*Why C is best.* It is specific, about her work, and true. People underestimate how good a compliment makes others feel and overestimate the awkwardness ([Boothby & Bohns 2021](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167220949003)).
*Why the others are weaker.* A is the most common choice and the one the research argues against. B is pleasant but forgettable. D starts well, then makes her reassure you.
*Note.* Keep work compliments about work, not appearance.
*Say it aloud.* "Compliment one specific thing she did."

#### 8. The forgotten name (small admission)

*Situation.* At the gym you meet Raj again. He says, "Hey, good to see you!" using your name. You've forgotten his. You remember he cycled to Brighton for charity.

| Option | Tier | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| A. Chat without using his name and hope it comes back | Flat | Bluffing (safety behaviour) |
| B. "Good to see you! I'm sorry, I've blanked on your name. I do remember the Brighton ride, though. How did it go?" | **Best** | Small admission plus callback |
| C. "Hey… you! How's it going?" | Misstep | Obvious cover-up |
| D. "Hi! Sorry, remind me of your name?" | Also good | Honest admission |

*Why B is best.* It admits a small slip, which others judge more kindly than we do ([Big Think on Bruk et al.](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/the-beautiful-mess-effect-other-people-view-our-vulnerability-more-positively-than-we-do)), and the specific memory shows he mattered.
*Why the others are weaker.* A keeps you anxious and distracted for the whole chat; hiding safety behaviours tend to backfire ([McManus et al. 2008](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:0abb384b-ee45-43ef-97be-6dbf43b8c3c7)). C fools no one. D is honest and fine, but B also shows you remember him.
*Say it aloud.* "Admit you've forgotten, and mention something you remember about him."

These eight items need keying by at least two more raters before release, per rule 5.

Updates: **body-mirroring** should cite the preregistered null ([Hale & Hamilton 2016](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/1531017/1/srep35295.pdf)). **talk-names** should say plainly that using names to be liked is unsupported. **charisma-two-parts** should add "show warmth before expertise in first meetings". Add a short "how this app trains you" card that says honestly that choosing the best reply is a warm-up, and speaking and real missions do the work.

### Connect: one new unit, and a predict-do-compare debrief everywhere

**Priority 1: change the mission debrief to predict, do, compare.** In the morning, the mission card asks one prediction: "How do you think it will go? How will they react?" (a 1–5 tap plus an optional line). In the evening, the debrief asks what happened, then shows the prediction beside the outcome: "You expected 2/5. It went 4/5." Over weeks, a small chart of predicted vs actual makes the gap visible. This copies the mechanism behind the mission study and behavioural experiments. Add one named safety behaviour to drop per mission ("Don't rehearse your next line; ask about theirs instead").

**Priority 1: add Unit 7, "Responding to news".** Teach active-constructive replies to good news and the understand-validate-care reply to bad news. Drill: the voice shares news; you reply in up to 20 seconds; notes check for a reaction, a specific question, and no shift or downside. Missions: "React to one piece of good news with a question about it" → "When someone shares a problem, ask one question before giving any advice" → "Thank someone for something specific."

**Priority 2: add disclosure to Unit 6, "Going deeper".** Add the depth ladder (fact → opinion → feeling → meaning): match their rung and go up at most one. Mission: "Share one honest opinion, then ask theirs."

**Priority 2: fix Unit 5's name mission** as described above.

**Priority 3: Unit 4's scenario cards** become the text scenario type below, with the spoken step.

### The text scenario question type

**Priority 1: build it as "choose, then say it".** Use the five-step format and four-tier grading above. Every item ends with a spoken step. Without that step, the item is recognition only, and the one trial of SJT training found no behaviour change.

**Priority 1: place it in the daily plan as the Connect warm-up**, not a separate lane. One to three items before the unit's voice drill, on the same principle. Example sequence for Unit 2: a scenario item on free information, then the existing "ask one follow-up" voice drill, then the mission.

**Priority 2: write the item bank by principle.** Four to six items per unit, each tagged with its principle and its distractor patterns. Key each with three raters. Start with the eight items here.

**Priority 2: schedule reviews.** Missed principles return in later days with new surface details (same move, new situation). Track first-try accuracy on unseen items per principle and aim for about 70%.

**Priority 3: optional extras.** The "Sure / Not sure" tap before reveal (flag confident misses as "worth a second look"). The warm-up guess on each unit's first item. A "most people chose" line once there is enough data, as On Cue does.

**What not to do.** No hearts or lives. No leaderboards. No claims that scenario items alone build charisma. No exact wording to memorise.

## Conclusion

The research shifts the question VoiceLab should ask of every feature. It is less "does the learner know the right move?" and more "did the learner *do* the move, out loud and then for real, and notice it went better than feared?" Knowing is cheap and fades. The warm acts that build connection are mostly ones people already half-know but avoid, because they overestimate the risk. That makes the predict-do-compare loop, not the quiz, the heart of the Connect lane.

Scenario questions still earn a place. They are fast, phone-friendly, and well suited to teaching the *names* of common mistakes, which gives learners a vocabulary for noticing their own habits. The honest framing is that they are a warm-up. VoiceLab can also produce evidence the field lacks: no study yet tracks whether app-based small-talk practice changes real conversations. Logged predictions, outcomes and first-try accuracy on new scenarios would let the app check its own claims rather than borrow them.
