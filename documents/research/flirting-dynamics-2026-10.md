# Clear, specific interest beats clever flirting

The science of flirting says that attraction grows most reliably when one person shows **clear interest that is plainly about this one person**, and backs it with **responsive attention**: follow-up questions, appreciation, empathy, and keeping the talk on the other person. Clever signals, games and tactics do much less. Nonverbal "tells" such as eye contact, smiling, nodding and closeness go with liking, but the effects are small (r ≈ .13–.21). They also look the same for friendly liking as for romantic interest, so no checklist can read a crush. People miss real flirting much more often than they spot it: in one lab study only **36% of men and 18% of women** noticed when their partner was flirting. Popular tactics fare badly. Uncertainty and "keep them guessing" are **mixed at best** and lower sexual desire in six studies. Negging has **no evidence that it works** and is seen as harmful. "36 questions make you fall in love" overstates a study that measured closeness, not love. The most useful finding for someone torn between flirting and going deeper is that **the two are not rivals**. The behaviours that predict "clicking" on a speed date are the same responsive behaviours that build closeness later. Playfulness helps when it carries clear interest and hurts when it hides it. For VoiceLab, this means the app can teach the core skills of good flirting without a dating persona: curiosity, specific appreciation, matched disclosure, noticing when someone wants out, and taking a soft no gracefully. These are everyday conversation skills, and the evidence for them is stronger than the evidence for anything flirting-specific.

**Evidence labels used throughout** (same as the earlier notes): **strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **moderate** (several studies, often from one lab, or one good trial), **mixed** (replications disagree), **weak** (one small study, a secondary report, or expert opinion), **myth** (tested and not supported, or no support found for a popular claim). ## Flirting signals are real but faint, and they don't separate romance from friendliness

The best evidence on nonverbal signals is a 2018 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis by Montoya, Kershaw and Prosser ([Murdoch record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analytic-investigation-of-the-relation/991005568569707891)). It links attraction to **closer distance (r = .21), more eye contact (r = .19), nodding (r = .16) and smiling (r = .13)**, according to press summaries ([Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-indicator-9008/)). These effects are real and small. The bigger finding is that the associations were **about the same size for platonic liking as for romantic interest**, so these cues show that someone likes you, not that they fancy you ([NatCom summary](https://www.natcom.org/publications-library/why-many-tell-tale-signs-attraction-are-likely-nonsense/)). The same analysis found **no link** between liking and hair flips, head tilts, eyebrow raises, open posture or leaning in, which are staples of "signs they like you" lists ([IFLScience](https://www.iflscience.com/editors-blog/these-are-the-signs-that-someone-is-attracted-to-you-according-to-a-new-study/)). **Strong** that the cues exist; **strong** that they are weak and non-specific.

That puts the classic observational work in context. Moore's 1985 bar study catalogued 52 "nonverbal solicitation signals" in women, including the darting glance and the hair flip. Women who signalled more were approached more, a reported fourfold difference ([Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com:443/us/blog/the-attraction-doctor/201706/11-key-flirting-techniques-women)). Being approached in a bar is a different outcome from felt attraction, and the study has not been formally replicated, so it is **weak to moderate**. Grammer and colleagues found that courtship-like behaviour peaks in the **first minute** of a stranger encounter. It only tracks women's stated interest from about **minute four onward**, so early signals are noise ([Göttingen page](https://psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grammeretal2000)). **Moderate, one lab.** Touch studies by Guéguen reported big jumps from a light forearm touch, with dance acceptance rising from 43% to 65% ([BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/power-light-touch-arm)). His body of work is now under integrity scrutiny, and several papers have been retracted or flagged ([Retraction Watch 2025](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/03/03/nicolas-gueguen-five-studies-bust-size-hitchhiking-retracted/)), so touch-as-tactic is **weak**.

Speech is more telling than body language. In nearly **1,000 four-minute Stanford speed dates**, women reported "clicking" with men who used **appreciative and sympathetic language** ("That's awesome", "That must be tough"). They also clicked with men who **interrupted to add to or finish their sentence** rather than to change topic. Both sexes clicked more when the **conversation centred on the woman** ([Stanford GSE](https://ed-old.gse.stanford.edu/news/study-speed-dating-what-makes-couples-click); [McFarland et al. 2013 PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf)). **Moderate**: one large corpus from one elite US graduate sample of heterosexual pairs, not replicated elsewhere. The same team trained a model to detect *intended* flirting. It reached 75% on familiar speakers but only **59% on new speakers against a 48% baseline**. Human partners were "very poor perceivers of intended flirtatiousness" and mostly **projected their own intent** onto the other person ([Ranganath et al. 2013 PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/RanganathEtAl2013_DetectingFlirting.pdf)). Flirting turns out to be personal in style, and people largely read their own feelings into it.

Humour works as a signal of interest as well as a display of quality. Across three studies, both men and women **joked more with, and found funnier, people they were already attracted to** ([Li et al. 2009, SMU](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/725)) (**moderate**). The well-replicated asymmetry is that **women value men who produce humour, and men value women who appreciate theirs** ([Bressler et al. 2006](https://abel.mcmaster.ca/publications/pdfs/Bressler%20et%20al..pdf); replicated by [Hone et al. 2015](https://doaj.org/article/721d10f1e06846d0a0bd40820fec57af)). **Strong** in Western student samples, untested elsewhere. Laughing at someone's jokes is therefore itself a flirting move. Playfulness, humour and being "fun-loving" rank near the top of desired partner traits in US and German-speaking student surveys ([Penn State](https://news.psu.edu/story/147649/2012/08/03/research/playfulness-may-help-adults-attract-mates-study-finds); [UZH](https://www.news.uzh.ch/en/articles/2015/-verspielte-erwachsene-werden-bei-der-partnerwahl-bevorzugt.html)). Those are *stated* preferences, though. No experiment found here tests whether playful teasing in a live encounter raises attraction, so that claim is **weak**. Teasing is also double-edged by design. Keltner's review defines it as "playful provocation" that relies on off-record cues such as tone or a wink, and it turns hostile when those cues are missing ([Keltner et al. 2001](https://esilab.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/Teasing-Review.pdf)).

Flirting is not always about romance either. Henningsen's six motives are **sex, relational, exploring, fun, esteem and instrumental**, and many flirts serve several at once. Men lean toward sexual motives and women toward relational ones ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flirting); [2008 workplace follow-up](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0021943608319390)). **Moderate.** This explains much of the misreading covered below. A flirt done for fun or a self-esteem boost looks the same as one done out of interest.

## Attraction builds on being chosen, not on being kept guessing

The most useful single idea in early attraction research is **selective reciprocity**. In Eastwick and colleagues' speed-dating study (N = 150), daters liked partners who desired them *more than those partners desired other people*. Daters who said yes to nearly everyone were **liked less in return**, and the authors read their indiscriminate interest as desperation ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/02/070206132059.htm); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/downside-having-too-much-love)). **Moderate**: a reanalysis by Gomez and Erber argued that matched attractiveness explains more. A larger point backs it up. When Joel, Eastwick and Finkel fed more than 100 pre-date traits into machine-learning models, they predicted how much people liked others in general and how liked people were in general. They predicted **essentially none of the dyad-specific "we click" part** ([APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/romantic-matches-are-hard-to-predict.html); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797617714580)). **Moderate.** Chemistry happens between two specific people and is built in the conversation. It cannot be found by screening in advance.

This rules out the idea that profile-matching produces attraction. A meta-analysis of 97 samples found that **stated ideals barely predict face-to-face attraction**. Physical attractiveness correlated about r = .40 with romantic evaluations for *both* sexes, and the sex differences people report on paper **shrank to almost nothing (r difference = .03) in person** ([UT Austin](https://news.utexas.edu/2013/06/18/review-of-research-calls-into-question-sex-differences-in-face-to-face-mate-preferences); [PubMed](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23586697/)). **Strong.**

"Playing hard to get" splits into two very different things, and the evidence treats them oppositely. Being **selective**, meaning seen as having options and standards, raises desirability. In three studies with a confederate, partners perceived as hard to get were rated higher in mate value and more desired, and participants worked harder to see them again ([Rochester](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/why-playing-hard-to-get-may-work-435602/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200608192510.htm)). Jonason and Li found that low or medium availability is preferred for **committed** partners, while high availability attracts **casual** interest ([WSU record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/playing-hard-to-get-manipulating-ones-perceived-availability-as-a/)). Both **moderate**. Making someone **uncertain whether you like them** is the opposite case. Whitchurch, Wilson and Gilbert's 2011 "he loves me, he loves me not" study found uncertain women more attracted, apparently because they thought about the man more ([author PDF](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Whitchurch%20et%20al%20(2011).pdf)). A later replication reportedly found no difference, and attraction *rose* when participants learned they were liked ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49694208_He_Loves_Me_He_Loves_Me_Not_Uncertainty_Can_Increase_Romantic_Attraction)). Birnbaum's six-study set found that uncertainty about a partner's interest **reduced sexual desire**, and a co-author called "uncertainty breeds desire" an idea "on life support" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218301870); [Futurity](https://www.futurity.org/?p=1777322)). The combined verdict on "keep them guessing" is **mixed, leaning unsupported**. Harry Reis's warning sums it up: if hard-to-get reads as disinterest or arrogance, "it will backfire."

**Responsiveness** (understanding, validating, caring; see the deep-dive note for the full frame) is the engine of desire *once a relationship exists*. In three studies it raised sexual desire, more so for women, because it made people feel special and made the partner seem a better mate ([Birnbaum et al. 2016](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/)). **Moderate.** At first meeting, the effect is gendered. Men saw a responsive woman as more attractive and more interested in them. Women did not reliably find a responsive male stranger more desirable, and Birnbaum suggests they may read him as "inappropriately nice" or eager to please ([Lehmiller summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2012/05/16/does-it-pay-to-be-a-nice-guy-in-the-long-run-yes-but-in-the); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/)). **Moderate, one lab group.** This fits the selectivity finding. Warmth aimed at everyone, or warmth that looks like trying too hard, signals a low bar. Warmth that is plainly about *this* person signals being chosen.

Asking questions sits on solid ground in getting-to-know-you chats. People who asked more **follow-up questions** were liked more, and in a speed-dating study they got more second-date yeses ([HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/it-helps-ask-cumulative-benefits-asking-follow-questions)). The online-chat result is **moderate**. The notes in this folder add a caveat that the earlier deep-dive did not include. Kluger and Malloy reanalysed the speed-dating data with the two-way structure of the dates modelled and reached different conclusions, and the original authors replied that their results hold ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/)). The *speed-dating* part should therefore be labelled **mixed**, not "strong–moderate" as in the earlier deep-dive. The advice to ask about their answer stands.

## Flirting and depth run on the same engine

The tension the user feels, between flirting and building something deeper, is weaker in the data than it feels in the moment. Hall's Flirting Styles Inventory (N = 5,020 online daters) found five styles: traditional, physical, sincere, playful and polite. **Sincere flirting**, meaning showing genuine emotional interest, and **physical flirting** went with **faster-forming relationships with more emotional connection and more chemistry** ([ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm)). Playful flirters said they flirt for fun and self-esteem. Coverage disagrees on whether playful flirting went with dating success, but it was the style **least linked to meaningful relationships** ([Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/11092-flirting-style-predicts-relationship-success-study-suggests.html)). **Moderate for the style structure, weak to moderate for outcomes**: the data are self-report, cross-sectional, from eHarmony users looking for long-term partners, and from one research group. No study found here *manipulated* playful versus sincere flirting and measured what followed.

The connection research completes the picture. Escalating, back-and-forth self-disclosure, as in Aron's "fast friends" procedure, reliably produces **more closeness than small talk** ([GGIA](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness)) (**strong** for closeness). Aron himself says it creates "a reasonable degree of closeness" quickly, not love. The romantic framing came from a 2015 *New York Times* essay ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/love-and-the-brain-part-1-the-36-questions-revisited)). Among strangers in live conversation, **more interaction produced more liking**, through perceived responsiveness, comfort and feeling that you know the person ([Reis et al. 2011](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf)) (**moderate**). Abstract trait lists, like profiles, can do the opposite. Learning more can expose differences and lower liking, and the two camps eventually published a joint model of when familiarity helps or hurts ([Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1057329)). *Perceived* similarity predicts attraction across all stages of a relationship, while the effect of *actual* similarity fades once people know each other ([Montoya et al. 2008](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Is-actual-similarity-necessary-for-attraction/991005635870107891)) (**strong**). This favours talking over screening.

Put together, these findings suggest a model. It is an inference from the studies above and has not been tested directly. **Attraction grows when one person signals interest that is selective and unambiguous, and pairs it with responsive attention.** Playfulness adds energy and lightness on top of that. It **undermines** connection in three cases: when it is aimed at everyone, when it creates real doubt about whether you are interested, or when it serves your own self-presentation more than attention to them. Under this model, flirting is how deeper connection *begins*, not a rival to it. The speed-date "click" behaviours (appreciation, empathy, collaborative interruptions, focus on the partner) are the same behaviours that sustain intimacy later. One speculative twist: because responsiveness from a stranger can read as eagerness, a light, playful register may be what lets early warmth land as confidence rather than neediness.

## People miss real flirting and mistake polite "no"s for "maybe"

Misreading runs in two directions at once. In the first, **men overperceive women's sexual interest and women underperceive men's**. This pattern dates to Abbey's 1982 lab study ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2744967)). It was confirmed in a within-person speed-meeting design (N = 199) by Perilloux, Easton and Buss, where overperception was strongest in men with a **short-term mating orientation**, men who rated themselves as **highly attractive**, and men meeting **attractive women** ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/all-it-takes-is-a-smile-for-some-guys.html)). It appears in self-reports from Norway (N = 308) and Japan, so it holds in both more and less gender-equal societies ([NTNU](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/2627053?show=full); [LEBS](https://lebs.hbesj.org/index.php/lebs/article/view/lebs.2016.47)). **Strong** for the direction of the effect, but no meta-analysis was found, and the cross-cultural work relies on remembered events. Error management theory explains it as an evolved bias toward the cheaper mistake. That explanation is a hypothesis, not a finding ([Uni Mannheim](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/sexuelles-selbstbewusstsein-eine-frage-des-geschlechts)).

In the second direction, **both sexes miss flirting that is actually happening**. In Hall, Xing and Brooks's study of 52 stranger pairs, people correctly spotted *no* flirting more than 80% of the time. When the partner *was* flirting, only **36% of men and 18% of women** noticed ([KU news](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2014/06/03/flirting-hard-detect-study-finds); [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/entities/publication/65a73d2d-0834-497c-a26e-5aadf7cf0b4a)). **Moderate, one lab.** The authors blame base rates: people assume flirting is rare and default to "not flirting." The two findings do not contradict each other. One measures *how much* interest is perceived. The other measures whether flirting is *detected at all*. Mood shifts both: after sexual priming, people saw potential partners as more attractive and more interested ([Neuroscience News](https://neurosciencenews.com/attraction-mindset-17072/amp/)).

Part of the cause is built into flirting. Indirect speech theory argues that veiled come-ons exist to give **plausible deniability**. A willing listener can take up the hint, and an unwilling one can let it pass without anyone losing face, while a direct line creates "common knowledge" that cannot be taken back ([Pinker et al. 2008, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2242675)). The theory is well cited but **weak to moderate** for flirting specifically. The implied trade-off is untested but plausible: the ambiguity that protects the person flirting is the same ambiguity that makes flirting so hard to detect.

The consent and rejection research turns this from a puzzle into a matter of safety. Consent is mostly signalled nonverbally. **Men rely more on nonverbal cues both to give and to read consent, and women more on verbal ones**, and each side believes it understands the other ([U. Arkansas](https://news.uark.edu/articles/21460/research-genders-communicate-consent-to-sex-differently)) (**moderate**). In one sample, over 43% of women rated every nonverbal behaviour as "not consent", compared with 20% of men ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32052687/)) (**weak to moderate**). On the rejection side, people **accept dates they would rather decline** when a real person's feelings are at stake ([Joel, Teper & MacDonald 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614552828)) (**moderate**). In two preregistered studies, suitors **underestimated how hard it was for targets to say no** and missed how much targets changed their routines to avoid them ([Bohns & DeVincent 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/xml/10.1177/1948550618769880)) (**moderate**). When safety worries are present, people choose ghosting over an explicit no ([Freedman et al. 2022](https://sites.olemiss.edu/scilab/publications/role-gender-and-safety-concerns-romantic-rejection-decisions)) (**moderate**, with results that varied between studies). These findings all point the same way: **hesitant, polite, delayed or vague responses are often disguised rejections, not invitations to try harder**. The practical rule, to slow down or ask plainly when signals are unclear, follows from this evidence but has not itself been tested.

Fear of rejection shapes who flirts at all. Starting contact is easier when people expect the attraction is shared ([Cornell ILR](https://archive.ilr.cornell.edu/news/about-ilr/romantic-rejection-research)). Teens high in rejection sensitivity at 16 were less likely to have a partner at 22 ([UVA](https://uva.theopenscholar.com/teenresearch/publications/accept-or-reject-impact-adolescent-rejection-sensitivity-early-adult)) (**moderate**). Online, people message others about **25% more desirable than themselves**, and women's average reply rate was below 20% ([MedicalXpress on Bruch & Newman 2018](https://m.medicalxpress.com/news/2018-08-game-online-dating-men-women.html)) (**moderate**). Women who send the first message are about **2.5 times as likely** to get a reply, according to OkCupid's company data ([TIME](https://time.com/4253826/women-okcupid-dating-apps/)) (**weak to moderate**). Low response rates are normal and not a verdict on the person.

Cultural evidence on flirting itself is **thin**. The misperception pattern holds across the US, Norway and Japan. US and Ukrainian women pursued similar goals with different nonverbal cues ([Annals of UCV](https://anale-lingvistica.reviste.ucv.ro/index.php/laucv/article/view/18)) (**weak to moderate**). A 2024 US/El Salvador abstract notes "little or no evidence" on cross-cultural flirting styles ([NEIU](https://neiudc.neiu.edu/srcas/2024/all/92)). Nearly every study covered here used heterosexual, mostly Western, often student samples, and LGBTQ+ flirting is essentially absent from this evidence. Claims like "culture X flirts directly" are mostly anecdote.

## What to do with this

### For someone torn between flirting and going deeper

The research does not ask you to choose. It suggests **flirting by going deeper, lightly**. The behaviours with the best evidence are sincere ones. Make your interest **specific and visible**: "I like talking to *you*," not general charm spread around the room. Ask about their answer, react with real appreciation, and keep the spotlight on them more than on you. Share something of your own at their depth, then hand the turn back. Use humour and playfulness as a sign of interest that rides on top of this, not as a smokescreen. Laughing at their jokes counts too. What the evidence warns against is **manufactured uncertainty**. Going quiet, playing it cool or teasing until they can't tell whether you're interested tends to cost desire rather than build it, and it pushes you toward being seen as a casual option rather than a serious one. Being selective is different. You can have a full life and standards *and* be clearly interested in this person.

Because flirting is hard to read in both directions, three habits protect both people. First, **don't trust tells**. A smile or eye contact means they like talking to you, which may or may not be romantic, and checklists of "signs" don't hold up. Second, **assume you are missing some real interest**, because most people do. A low-cost, direct step such as "I'd like to do this again, would you?" resolves more than decoding does. Third, **treat a vague, delayed or polite-but-flat response as a probable no**. People find it hard to refuse, accept dates to be kind, and use soft exits when they feel unsafe. Easing off gracefully is both respectful and, as the evidence suggests, the least costly mistake.

### For VoiceLab

The competitor review advised against dating or flirting personas, for good reasons: it is a crowded, criticised corner, and a peer was fined by a regulator. This evidence supports that decision for a further reason. **What makes flirting work is mostly ordinary conversation skill with clear intent added**, and VoiceLab already teaches, or plans to teach, the skill part. The app can serve learners who want this without simulating romance.

**Priority 1: label the overlap without a persona.** Add a short Learn card set, perhaps titled "Interested in someone?", within the existing myth-or-fact and conversation sets. Its messages: *specific interest beats general charm* (moderate); *the click comes from appreciation, empathy and keeping it about them* (moderate); *keeping them guessing usually backfires* (mixed, leaning unsupported); *body-language "signs" can't read a crush* (myth); *negging doesn't work* (myth); *36 questions build closeness, not love* (overclaim). These cards point back to the same drills (follow-ups, responding to news, matching depth) that the deep-dive recommended. A learner who wants flirting help therefore practises the same everyday moves, framed for their goal.

**Priority 1: teach reading disinterest and taking a soft no, in everyday settings.** The rejection findings carry the highest stakes for safety and transfer well to non-romantic settings: a colleague who keeps giving short answers, a friend who says "maybe" to plans three times. The existing "Shorter answers" scenario already models a warm exit. Add one or two items where the best answer treats hesitation as a no and leaves the door open without pushing, plus a scenario about asking directly instead of guessing. This also covers consent-adjacent judgement without sexual content.

**Priority 2: a "playful, not ambiguous" note in feedback.** When the live partner hears teasing, the coach can check the off-record cues teasing depends on: was the tone clearly light, and was warmth stated as well? This stays in everyday contexts, such as banter with a friend or coworker. It does not grade "flirtiness".

**Priority 2: update one existing label.** The deep-dive marks follow-up questions **strong–moderate**. Given the Kluger and Malloy reanalysis, the *speed-dating* claim should read **moderate (online chats); mixed (speed dating)**. The behavioural advice stays the same.

**What not to do.** Do not build an AI partner who signals attraction, rewards persistence or "warms up" after the user pushes past a no, because that trains exactly the misreading the evidence warns about. Do not score learners on "reading signals" from voice cues: even trained models manage 59% on new speakers. Do not teach pickup-style tactics, scripts or uncertainty games. If VoiceLab ever reconsiders a dating-adjacent mode, the evidence points to a **first-meeting-with-someone-you-like** scenario that scores responsiveness and clear, specific interest, and in which the AI sometimes declines politely so the learner practises taking a no well. A flirting simulator would not fit this evidence.

## Conclusion

Popular advice treats flirting as a code: signals to send, tells to read and games to win. The evidence points the other way. The signals are faint, the tells don't separate romance from friendliness, and the games mostly cost desire. What predicts attraction is what predicts connection in general: attention that is responsive and clearly *about this person*, a little playfulness, and willingness to say plainly what you want. The person torn between flirting and depth is facing a smaller choice than it feels. Sincere interest, shown with some lightness, is both.

Two gaps stand out. First, almost no experiment has manipulated a flirting style and followed the relationships that resulted, and nearly all of the data are heterosexual, Western and from students, so confident claims about "what works" outrun the evidence. Second, the biggest practical risk in this area is misreading a quiet no, not failing to flirt well enough. A practice app that teaches people to notice and honour reluctance in everyday talk would do more good than one that teaches them to be smoother. VoiceLab can build that version while staying clear of the dating corner of the market.
