# Early romantic attraction: how interest builds and how flirting relates to deeper connection

Evidence labels: **strong** (meta-analysis or several independent replications), **moderate** (several studies, often one lab, or one good trial), **mixed** (replications disagree), **weak** (one small study, secondary report, or expert opinion), **myth** (tested and not supported).

Method note: about 18 web searches, no full-text PDF reads. Several details come from search-result summaries of abstracts, press releases and secondary write-ups. Treat exact numbers as needing a check against the original papers before anyone quotes them publicly. Notes on how sure each finding is appear inline.

## 1. Reciprocity of liking in romance (selective vs unselective desire; social relations model; Montoya & Insko)

### Takeaway
Being liked back attracts people only when the liking feels *unique* to them. A dater who says yes to almost everyone is liked *less*. Most of the "who likes whom" signal is dyadic (relationship variance), and pre-meeting traits cannot predict it.

### Cited Findings
- **(moderate)** Eastwick, Finkel, Mochon & Ariely (2007), *Psychological Science* 18(4), 317–319. N = 150 undergraduates; each had 4-minute speed dates with 9–13 opposite-sex partners. Daters liked partners who desired them *more than that partner desired other people* ("reciprocal unique desire"). Daters who desired nearly everyone were desired less in return. The authors suggest that unselective romantic interest reads as desperation. Peer replication is limited; the main critique is a reanalysis (next bullet) — [ScienceDaily release](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2007/02/070206132059.htm); [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/810358); [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/downside-having-too-much-love)
- **(weak, critique)** Gomez & Erber (2013) reanalysed the data. They argued that how closely the daters' attractiveness matched was the strongest predictor of desire, and they called the "broadcasting unselectivity" explanation "deeply dissatisfying". Found through search summaries; I could not open a primary link — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/downside-having-too-much-love)
- **(moderate)** A secondary summary of the selective/unselective line of work: only having eyes for one date creates "chemistry" — [Psychology Today (Why Bad Looks Good blog)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/why-bad-looks-good/201707/how-only-having-eyes-for-your-date-creates-chemistry)
- **(moderate)** Social-relations-model split of desire (Joel, Eastwick & Finkel 2017; two speed-dating studies with more than 100 pre-date measures each). Pre-date traits predicted 4–18% of *actor* variance (how much a person likes others in general) and 7–27% of *partner* variance (how much a person is liked in general). They predicted essentially none of the *relationship* variance (unique liking within a dyad) — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/romantic-matches-are-hard-to-predict.html); [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797617714580); [author PDF via gwern](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/2017-joel.pdf)
- **(weak, secondary attribution)** Montoya & Insko (2008), as described in a review: similarity-induced attraction rests on reciprocal liking (perceiving that the other person likes you), and the reciprocity effect comes from inferring that the other person has warm intentions toward you — [Interpersona review (PsychOpen)](https://interpersona.psychopen.eu/index.php/interpersona/article/download/3377/3377.html?inline=1)

### Inferences
- In romance, "liking back" works as a signal of *being chosen*. Generalised warmth toward everyone does not carry that signal. This fits Joel et al.: the active part of mutual attraction is dyad-specific.
- For flirting, this means attention that is visibly specific to one person ("I like *you*") should do better than indiscriminate charm.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Kenny's own SRM papers or speed-dating SRM variance decompositions beyond Joel et al. 2017. The Eastwick 2007 reciprocity analyses were also SRM-based, but I did not confirm their exact coefficients.
- I did not retrieve the Montoya & Insko (2008, *European Journal of Social Psychology*) primary text, sample sizes or effect sizes. The only source found is a secondary attribution.
- I found no direct independent replication of the 2007 selective/unselective result.

## 2. Uncertainty and playing hard to get

### Takeaway
"He loves me, he loves me not" uncertainty raised attraction in one well-known study (Whitchurch et al. 2011). The effect did not replicate cleanly, and Birnbaum's work finds that uncertainty usually *dampens* sexual desire. Being perceived as hard to get (selective, high mate value) can raise desirability. Seeming disinterested backfires. Overall rating: **mixed**.

### Cited Findings
- **(mixed)** Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert (2011), *Psychological Science* 22(2), 172–175. Female undergraduates (N about 47 in the original paper; I did not confirm N in the sources found) viewed the Facebook profiles of men said to have seen *their* profiles. Women who were *uncertain* whether the men liked them were more attracted than women told the men liked them "a lot". The proposed mechanism was that uncertain women thought about the men more — [UVA Batten summary](https://batten.virginia.edu/he-loves-me-he-loves-me-not-uncertainty-can-increase-romantic-attraction); [author PDF](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Whitchurch%20et%20al%20(2011).pdf); [Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-09e0-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)
- **(mixed)** A later replication attempt (Montoya et al., 2015, as summarised in search results) found no significant difference in attraction between the certain-liking and uncertain conditions. In a within-subject design, attraction *rose* when participants became certain they were liked. I could not open a primary link for this study; it surfaced alongside — [ResearchGate page for Whitchurch et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/49694208_He_Loves_Me_He_Loves_Me_Not_Uncertainty_Can_Increase_Romantic_Attraction)
- **(weak)** An Ohio University thesis tested target uncertainty with a more self-relevant measure. The original model did not replicate, but "wondering" about the target's opinion predicted later attraction — [OhioLINK ETD "Target-Uncertainty and Initial Romantic Attraction"](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=ohiou1375118131&disposition=inline)
- **(weak)** A student replication modelled on Whitchurch in a Tinder-style setting (with pupil dilation) exists. I could not verify its outcome — [CORE PDF "Playing hard to get: attraction, uncertainty, and Tinder"](https://pdf.core.ac.uk/corefilesystem/pdf/3065/345084614.pdf)
- **(moderate)** Birnbaum et al. (2018), "Are you into me? Uncertainty and sexual desire in online encounters and established relationships", *Computers in Human Behavior*. Coverage reports six studies (experiments plus daily diaries). Uncertainty about a partner's interest *reduced* sexual desire for that partner. The authors frame desire as a gut-level signal of mate suitability, inhibited to protect the self from investing in an uncertain prospect. One co-author said the "uncertainty breeds desire" idea is "on life support" — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218301870); [Rochester news: why certainty is good for romance](https://www.futurity.org/?p=1777322)
- **(moderate)** Birnbaum, Reis and colleagues (2020), *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships*, three studies. Participants interacted with an opposite-sex confederate. Partners *perceived* as hard to get were rated higher in mate value and were more sexually desired, and participants put more concrete effort into seeing them again. Reis cautioned that if hard-to-get comes across as disinterest or arrogance, "it will backfire". I did not confirm the author list beyond Birnbaum and Reis — [University of Rochester news](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/why-playing-hard-to-get-may-work-435602/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/2020/06/new-study-sheds-light-on-how-and-why-being-hard-to-get-can-make-you-seem-more-desirable-56971); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/06/200608192510.htm)
- **(moderate)** Jonason & Li (2013), *European Journal of Personality* 27, 458–469, four studies at four US universities. They catalogued hard-to-get tactics (a reported 58 behaviours, e.g. acting busy). Highly *available* targets were preferred as casual-sex partners. Medium- or low-availability targets were preferred for committed relationships, and participants said they would spend more time and money on low-availability targets — [Western Sydney University record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/playing-hard-to-get-manipulating-ones-perceived-availability-as-a/); [SMU record](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/1476); [Sex and Psychology summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2013/03/20/does-playing-hard-to-get-pay-off)

### Inferences
- The two lines of work fit together if "hard to get" means *selective* and "uncertain" means *unsure whether I am wanted*. Selectivity combined with clear interest in *this* person combines the hard-to-get finding with the selective-desire finding (section 1). Deliberate ambiguity about one's interest is more likely to suppress desire.
- "Keep them guessing" as general advice is best labelled **mixed, leaning unsupported**. The claim that uncertainty reliably increases attraction is close to **myth** status for sexual desire (Birnbaum 2018). I would not call it a full myth, because Whitchurch's thought-occupation effect has not been cleanly refuted.

### Gaps
- I found no large preregistered or multi-lab replication of Whitchurch et al. (2011).
- I could not verify sample sizes for the Birnbaum 2018/2020 studies or for Jonason & Li.

## 3. Responsiveness and desire

### Takeaway
In established relationships, a partner's responsiveness (understanding, validating, caring) increases sexual desire, especially in women, because it makes one feel special and makes the partner seem a better mate. In *first* encounters the effect depends on gender and attachment style: men tend to read a responsive woman as interested and desirable, while some women read a responsive stranger as overly eager or manipulative. Overall rating: **moderate**, mostly from one lab group.

### Cited Findings
- **(moderate)** Birnbaum, Reis, Mizrahi, Kanat-Maymon, Sass & Granovski-Milner (2016), "Intimately connected", *JPSP* 111(4), 530–546, three studies (including experiments and a diary study). Responsiveness increased desire, more strongly for women. The link ran through feeling special and seeing the partner as a valuable mate — [Bar-Ilan CRIS record](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/); [Ben-Gurion CRIS record](https://cris.bgu.ac.il/en/publications/intimately-connected-the-importance-of-partner-responsiveness-for/); [ISRAEL21c coverage](https://www.israel21c.org/?p=79656)
- **(moderate)** Birnbaum & Reis (2012), *PSPB* 38(7), 946–958, "When does responsiveness pique sexual interest? Attachment and sexual desire in initial acquaintanceships". In initial encounters, men perceived a responsive woman as more feminine and attractive and as more sexually interested in them. Women did not reliably find a responsive man more desirable; anxiously attached women were the exception and found responsiveness appealing. Birnbaum's interpretation is that women may see a responsive stranger as "inappropriately nice", manipulative or eager to please — [citation in Cambridge Core reference list](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/C068B21897A1B25EC553971FFFC2C628); [Sex and Psychology (Lehmiller) summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2012/05/16/does-it-pay-to-be-a-nice-guy-in-the-long-run-yes-but-in-the); [BioSpace/Rochester release](https://www.biospace.com/why-men-like-nice-women-and-why-women-like-bad-boys-university-of-rochester-study). (The attachment-moderation detail is from my prior knowledge of the abstract; the sources found confirm only the gendered pattern.)
- **(moderate)** Birnbaum, Ein-Dor, Reis & Segal (2014), "Why do men prefer nice women?" is the follow-up showing that men's attraction to responsive women runs through perceiving them as feminine and as interested — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/men-prefer-nice-women-responsiveness-desire/); [HuffPost coverage](https://www.huffingtonpost.ca/2014/07/28/study-decodes-responsive-partners_n_5626295.html)
- **(moderate)** Reis et al. (2011), *JPSP*: in getting-acquainted conversations, perceived responsiveness was one of three mediators (with comfort and perceived knowledge) explaining why more interaction produced more liking — [Reis lab PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- Responsiveness is the most robust bridge from flirting to deeper connection once a relationship exists. Early on, it probably needs to come with signals of selectivity or self-respect so that it is not read as eagerness to please. This matches the "unselective = desperate" finding in sections 1–2.

### Gaps
- I found no independent-lab replication of the gendered first-encounter responsiveness effect, and no meta-analysis of responsiveness and *initial* attraction.

## 4. Speed-dating findings: question-asking, stated vs revealed preferences, predictability, "clicking"

### Takeaway
What people *say* they want in a partner predicts their face-to-face attraction poorly. Attractiveness matters for both sexes, and the sex differences seen on paper largely disappear in person. Compatibility cannot be predicted from pre-meeting traits. Conversational *behaviour* does predict liking and "clicking": follow-up questions, appreciative and empathic responses, engaged interruptions, and a focus on the partner.

### Cited Findings
- **(moderate; speed-dating part contested)** Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), *JPSP*, "It doesn't hurt to ask". Studies 1–2 were online chats with more than 600 participants. Study 3 had 110 speed-daters and about 2,000 conversations. Asking more questions, especially follow-up questions, increased liking, and speed-daters who asked more follow-ups got more second-date "yes"es. People did not predict this effect — [HKS publication page](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/it-helps-ask-cumulative-benefits-asking-follow-questions); [author PDF via gwern](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/novelty/2017-huang.pdf); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/asking-questions-can-get-you-a-better-job-or-a-second-date)
- **(mixed)** Kluger & Malloy (2019) reanalysed the speed-dating data with the asymmetric dyadic structure modelled and reached different conclusions. Huang et al. replied that their results are robust. The online-chat effect is less disputed — [Harvard DASH (reply/critique records)](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/42704282); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6857721/)
- **(strong)** Eastwick, Luchies, Finkel & Hunt (2014), *Psychological Bulletin*, meta-analysis of 97 samples. Physical attractiveness correlated r ≈ .40 with romantic evaluations and earning prospects r ≈ .10, for both sexes. Sex differences were tiny (r difference = .03) and non-significant in face-to-face contexts. Stated sex-differentiated ideals did not produce sex-differentiated responses to live partners — [UT Austin news](https://news.utexas.edu/2013/06/18/review-of-research-calls-into-question-sex-differences-in-face-to-face-mate-preferences); [Kellogg record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2014/the-predictive-validity-of-ideal-partner-preferences-a-review/); [PubMed](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/23586697/)
- **(moderate)** Joel, Eastwick & Finkel (2017): random-forest models built on more than 100 self-report traits and preferences could predict general desirability but not dyad-specific desire. Eastwick compares romantic desire to an earthquake (a chaotic, dynamic process) rather than a chemical reaction — [APS release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/romantic-matches-are-hard-to-predict.html); [UK Data Service ReShare dataset](https://reshare.ukdataservice.ac.uk/852716)
- **(moderate)** McFarland, Jurafsky & Rawlings (2013), "Making the connection: Social bonding in courtship situations", *American Journal of Sociology* 118(6), 1596–1649, nearly 1,000 transcribed heterosexual speed dates. Women reported "clicking" with men who used appreciative and empathic language ("That's awesome", "That must be tough") and who interrupted to show engagement rather than to change the topic. Both sexes reported clicking when the conversation centred on the woman — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed.stanford.edu/node/1819); [author PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~lpearl/courses/readings/McFarlandEyAl2013_SocialBonding.pdf); [Duke record](https://fds.duke.edu/db/aas/Sociology/faculty/craig.rawlings/publications/337990)

### Inferences
- Taken together: you cannot screen for chemistry in advance, but behaviour *during* the interaction (curiosity, follow-ups, warmth, attention to the other person) is where attraction is built. This gives concrete, trainable behaviours for a conversation-prompt product.
- "Matching on stated preferences produces attraction" is best labelled close to **myth** for face-to-face attraction, given Eastwick 2014 and Joel 2017.

### Gaps
- The McFarland et al. data come from one speed-dating event series at one university, and I found no independent replication.
- I found no 2020–2026 large-scale replication of the follow-up-question effect in romantic settings specifically.

## 5. Self-disclosure and closeness; Aron's 36 questions and replications

### Takeaway
Escalating, reciprocal self-disclosure (Aron et al. 1997, "fast friends") reliably produces more closeness than small talk in a lab session. The effect is about *closeness*, not falling in love. Replications support the closeness effect, and video chat appears to work like face-to-face.

### Cited Findings
- **(strong for closeness vs small talk)** Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator (1997), *PSPB*, "The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness". Pairs randomly assigned to escalating self-disclosure questions felt significantly closer than small-talk pairs. Sources disagree on the session length (45 min vs about 1.5 h), and the popularised "36 questions" list differs from the original set of about 40 questions Aron supplied — [PsychCentral](https://psychcentral.com/blog/want-to-be-close-to-someone-ask-these-36-questions); [gwern-hosted note on the original question set](https://gwern.net/doc/www/localhost/09b0a98c768be0ee6fd46fabd64494e1056eb628.html); [Greater Good in Action practice](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/practice_as_pdf/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness)
- **(weak, as sourced here)** A Sprecher (2021) replication reportedly found the closeness effect with no difference between face-to-face and video chat. I found this only through a study-aid page and did not verify it — [Brainscape flashcards](https://brainscape.com/flashcards/communication-13371567/packs/21384784)
- **(weak)** Aron himself says the procedure creates "a reasonable degree of closeness" quickly, not love. The romance framing comes from a 2015 NYT essay — [Scientific American podcast](https://www.scientificamerican.com/podcast/episode/love-and-the-brain-part-1-the-36-questions-revisited); [ARU news](https://aru.ac.uk/news/can-36-questions-really-change-your-love-life)
- **(moderate)** The mechanism is *reciprocal* self-disclosure that signals willingness to be responsive. This is consistent with the responsiveness mediator in Reis et al. 2011 — [Reis lab PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf)

### Inferences
- "36 questions make strangers fall in love" is best labelled a **myth/overclaim**, since the study measured closeness, not love. "Escalating mutual disclosure builds closeness" is well supported.
- The *gradual escalation* and *turn-taking* design is what matters. For a product, this means paced, reciprocal prompts rather than one-sided deep questions.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the Sprecher replication papers or any multi-lab/registered replication. I know Sprecher's lab has published several Aron-procedure studies (e.g. on reciprocal vs non-reciprocal disclosure), but the searches did not surface primary sources, so they are not cited here.
- I found no romantic-outcome follow-up (e.g. dating rates) of 36-questions pairs.

## 6. Similarity vs complementarity; familiarity / mere exposure

### Takeaway
Similarity predicts attraction (meta-analysis), and *perceived* similarity matters more than actual similarity once people interact. In live conversation, more interaction generally increases liking (Reis et al. 2011). Learning *more* about someone can reduce liking when the new information reveals dissimilarity (Norton et al. 2007). The two camps later published a joint integrative model. Evidence for complementarity in attraction is weak; none was found in this search.

### Cited Findings
- **(strong)** Montoya, Horton & Kirchner (2008), *JSPR* 25(6), 889–922, meta-analysis of 313 studies and 460 effect sizes. Actual similarity r = .47 and perceived similarity r = .39 with attraction. Perceived similarity predicted attraction across no-interaction, short-interaction and existing-relationship studies, while actual similarity's effect weakened in existing relationships — [Murdoch research portal](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Is-actual-similarity-necessary-for-attraction/991005635870107891); [Psychology Today (Attraction Doctor)](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-attraction-doctor/201709/does-similarity-lead-to-attraction-and-compatibility)
- **(moderate)** Reis, Maniaci, Caprariello, Eastwick & Finkel (2011), *JPSP*. In live interactions between strangers, more interaction led to more liking, mediated by perceived responsiveness, comfort and perceived knowledge — [Reis lab PDF](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_2011.pdf); [Finkel lab PDF](https://faculty.wcas.northwestern.edu/eli-finkel/documents/InPress-a_ReisManiaciCaprarielloEastwickFinkel_JPSP.pdf)
- **(moderate)** Norton, Frost & Ariely (2007, *JPSP*, "Less is more"; title from prior knowledge, not confirmed by search). More information about a target, drawn randomly from trait lists, increased perceived dissimilarity and therefore disliking ("familiarity breeds contempt"). Their 2011 comment argued that Reis et al.'s paradigms favoured a familiarity–liking link — [VU Amsterdam record of comment](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/does-familiarity-breed-contempt-or-liking-comment-on-reis-maniaci/); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/810345)
- **(moderate, theory)** Finkel, Norton, Reis, Ariely, Caprariello, Eastwick, Frost & Maniaci (2015), *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10(1), 3–19: an adversarial-collaboration integrative model of when familiarity promotes vs undermines attraction — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1057329)

### Inferences
- In a real conversation, getting to know someone usually *helps*, because responsiveness and comfort grow. Abstract trait information (as in profiles) can *hurt*, because it exposes dissimilarities. This favours conversation-first over profile-first contact.
- "Opposites attract" (complementarity) is best labelled **weak or unsupported** for initial attraction. I did not retrieve a direct test in this search, so this is an inference rather than a sourced verdict.

### Gaps
- I did not search for complementarity studies (e.g. dominance/submissiveness) or Montoya & Horton's later attitude-similarity work, so there are no primary sources on complementarity here.

## 7. How playful or flirtatious behaviour and sincere connection combine, and when one undermines the other

### Takeaway
The flirting-style evidence base is thin and mostly self-report. In Hall's flirting-styles work, *sincere* flirting (expressing genuine emotional interest) and *physical* flirting were linked to deeper relationships, while *playful* flirting (fun, ego-boosting) was linked to fewer meaningful relationships. Combined with the experimental work above, playfulness seems to help when it carries clear, selective interest and to undermine connection when it signals indiscriminate interest, ambiguity or ego-play.

### Cited Findings
- **(weak to moderate; cross-sectional self-report)** Hall et al. (2010), more than 5,000 online-dating users (mean age about 40), 26-item measure. Five styles: physical, traditional, polite, sincere, playful. Sincere and physical styles were associated with faster-forming, more meaningful relationships with emotional connection and chemistry. Playful flirters said they flirt for fun and self-esteem, and playful flirting was least associated with meaningful relationships. Outlets disagree on whether playful flirting was associated with dating success at all. Gender differences within styles were small — [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/11/101101082901.htm); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/11092-flirting-style-predicts-relationship-success-study-suggests.html); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20101101/Understanding-flirting-key-to-positive-romantic-interactions.aspx)
- **(moderate)** Unselective interest is penalised (Eastwick 2007), and hard-to-get backfires when read as disinterest or arrogance (Reis, 2020 coverage). Both mark limits on "game-playing" flirtation — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/downside-having-too-much-love); [University of Rochester news](https://www.rochester.edu/newscenter/why-playing-hard-to-get-may-work-435602/)
- **(moderate)** Uncertainty about a partner's interest lowers sexual desire (Birnbaum 2018). Ambiguous, teasing flirtation that leaves interest unclear may therefore cost desire — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563218301870)
- **(moderate)** Behaviours that predict "clicking" (appreciation, empathy, engaged interruptions, partner focus) and liking (follow-up questions) are sincere-attention behaviours, not tactics — [Stanford GSE news](https://ed.stanford.edu/node/1819); [HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/it-helps-ask-cumulative-benefits-asking-follow-questions)
- **(moderate)** Jonason & Li (2013): high availability attracts *casual* interest, while lower availability is preferred for *committed* partners. "Easy" playful flirtation may therefore position someone as a short-term rather than a long-term prospect — [Sex and Psychology summary](https://www.sexandpsychology.com/blog/2013/03/20/does-playing-hard-to-get-pay-off)

### Inferences
- A synthesis model, inferred and not directly tested: attraction grows when one person signals **selective, unambiguous interest** together with **responsive attention** (questions, appreciation, reciprocal disclosure). Playfulness adds energy when it rides on that signal. It undermines connection when it (a) is indiscriminate, (b) creates real uncertainty about interest, or (c) serves self-presentation rather than attention to the other person.
- Responsiveness can feel "too nice" to some women at first meeting (Birnbaum & Reis 2012). A light, playful register may help keep warmth from reading as eagerness. This is speculative.

### Gaps
- I found no experiments that manipulate *playful vs sincere* flirting and measure attraction or relationship formation. Hall's primary paper (and the Flirting Styles Inventory validation) was not retrieved, so sample details and effect sizes are unverified.
- I did not cover the literature on humour in attraction (e.g. humour production vs appreciation, Bressler et al.) or the communication-studies literature on flirting ambiguity (Henningsen). Both are relevant next searches.
