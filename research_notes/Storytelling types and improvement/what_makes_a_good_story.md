# What makes a good spoken, true, everyday story

Scope: short (1-5 minute) true personal stories told out loud by ordinary people (conversation, work, toasts, Moth-style stages). Grading used throughout:
- **Strong**: meta-analysis or replicated experiments
- **Moderate**: a single good study, or established sociolinguistics built on large corpora of real oral narratives
- **Practical**: expert practice with no formal evidence

Method note: the web search tool worked, but direct page fetches were blocked by the session's network allow-list (themoth.org, niemanstoryboard.org, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and responsebasedpractice.com all failed to resolve). So the findings below come from search-result abstracts, publisher and index pages, and secondary summaries, not full texts. Where a claim rests on a secondary summary, it says so. To check exact effect sizes, those hosts would need to be allowed.

## Structure: what shapes do told personal stories take, and which have evidence?

### Takeaway
Labov & Waletzky's model is the only structure on the list that was derived from real oral personal stories. Its central claims are well established linguistics (moderate), though the model is now thought too rigid: the parts do not always appear, or appear in that order. Freytag, the Story Spine, but/therefore and Dicks's five-second moment are practitioner tools with no direct experimental test (practical), but they all point the same way. A told story needs a causal chain that leads to one most reportable event, or moment of change, and an evaluation that says why that event matters.

### Cited Findings
- **Labov & Waletzky (1967), with Labov (1997) [Moderate].** Oral narratives of personal experience break into abstract, orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution and coda. Evaluation is the part where the narrator signals why the story is worth telling. Evaluation is "typically placed between the complicating action and the resolution, which creates suspense before the outcome," and the coda returns the listener to the present. — [Uni Łódź Press chapter on Labov](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1); [Berkeley course reading, Labov "Narrative"](https://blogs.ischool.berkeley.edu/i290-mn-f13/files/2013/09/narrative.pdf)
- **Most reportable event (MRE) [Moderate].** Labov (1997) defines a narrative as a sequence of actions that builds to a peak event. The MRE is "the point of the story": the most unusual event, with the greatest emotional impact on narrator and audience. The narrator builds the story by working back along the causal chain from the MRE to the situation that triggered it. — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1); [Labov, "Narratives of ordinary events" (PDF)](https://www.nu.edu.ye/wp-content/uploads/2014/05/narrative-ordinary-events-labov.pdf)
- **Evaluation can be spread through a story [Moderate].** Evaluation can attach to one event, a group of events or the whole story. One evaluated event is often marked with an irrealis clause (a conditional or hypothetical, such as "I could have died"). — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **Critique: the model is too rigid [Moderate].** Later scholars note that evaluation "may in some cases be explicit, and may be located prior to the resolution, but the expression of the evaluation within a narrative need not take this form." — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **Ordinary events have a job too [Moderate].** In Labov's later work (2013), many events in a story are not themselves reportable and are not part of the causal chain, yet they still convey what the experience was like for the narrator. — [LINGUIST List review 25.3040](https://linguistlist.org/issues/25/3040/)
- **Why Labov collected these stories [Moderate].** Labov collected personal-experience narratives on highly reportable topics (danger of death, moral indignation) because people telling them shift into vernacular, engaged speech. This is the origin of the "danger of death" prompt. — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **But/therefore [Practical].** Trey Parker and Matt Stone (NYU session, dated 2011 in some sources and 2014 in others) say that if "and then" fits between two story beats, the beats should be rewritten so they connect with "but" or "therefore." Their writers' room writes this chain out on a whiteboard. Commentators note that "and then" is not always wrong. — [Wikipedia, Transition (fiction)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_(fiction)); [The Hustle](https://thehustle.co/storytelling-secrets-netted-matt-stone-trey-parker-600-million); [No Film School / Tony Zhou](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/04/tony-zhou-storytelling-video-essay-f-fake-therefore-but-and-then); [Perell](https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/)
- **Story Spine [Practical].** Kenn Adams created it for improv: late 1980s according to NPR, 1991 according to other sources. It has eight starters: "Once upon a time… Every day… But one day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… And ever since then…". Rebecca Stockley later taught improv to Pixar's writers, which is how the spine became linked with Pixar, but Adams says Pixar's success does not depend on it. Teachers map it onto three acts: setup ("every day"), middle ("because of that") and resolution ("ever since then"). — [NPR via WUNC, 2026](https://www.wunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories); [Improv Basics: Story Spine (Substack)](https://sidrdesai.substack.com/p/improv-basics-story-spine)
- **Dicks's five-second moment [Practical].** Every story worth telling is built around one brief moment of change, which Dicks says takes about five seconds. Without one, a story is "an anecdote." Work out the ending first, then the meaning of the five-second moment, then open on the opposite (end confident, start in doubt; end in love, start lonely). — [Shortform summary of Storyworthy](https://www.shortform.com/summary/storyworthy-summary-matthew-dicks); [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/); [Lenny's Newsletter interview with Dicks](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew)
- **Moth: build in scenes [Practical].** Moth stories rely on scenes, and the climax is usually a scene. The Moth's book covers chronological and flashback structures. — [Reader notes on *How to Tell a Story* (The Moth, 2022)](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/59000869-how-to-tell-a-story/7552515-erhan?page=2); [book record and chapter list](https://repository.museumsiam.org/handle/6622252777/888)
- **Freytag's pyramid:** I found no evidence that it applies to short oral personal stories. It comes from five-act drama. (See Gaps.)

### Inferences
- The frameworks line up. Labov's complicating action matches the spine's "But one day / because of that" and but/therefore's causal links. Labov's MRE matches Dicks's five-second moment and the spine's "until finally." Labov's evaluation and coda match Dicks's "meaning of the moment" and the spine's "ever since then." An app can teach one skeleton and draw on all of them: **a normal before, something that changed it, a causal chain (but/therefore), one peak moment, what it meant and what is different now.**
- Evaluation is the part most missing from untrained stories and most valued by experts (the Moth's "stakes," Dicks's "meaning"). It is the best target for coaching feedback.
- Labov's "ordinary events" finding gives a sensible limit on how hard to cut. Keep a small amount of non-causal detail that conveys what it felt like. Cut detail that does neither job.

### Gaps
- I found no experiment comparing Freytag, the Story Spine or but/therefore against each other or against a control for oral stories. Their value rests on practice.
- I could not get Labov & Waletzky (1967) or Labov (1997) as full texts (fetch blocked). The claims come from secondary academic summaries.

## Engagement: what makes listeners engaged and moved?

### Takeaway
Narrative transportation is the best-evidenced engagement mechanism (strong). Its levers are characters listeners can identify with, a plot that evokes vivid imagery, and events that feel plausible. Stories produce small-to-moderate persuasion effects (strong), are easier to recall and understand than exposition (strong, from one meta-analysis), and showing vulnerability is judged more kindly by others than by the teller (moderate to strong: seven studies in one paper). Constructed dialogue, repetition and imagery are the involvement strategies Tannen documents in real talk (moderate).

### Cited Findings
- **van Laer et al. (2014) meta-analysis [Strong].** *Journal of Consumer Research* 40(5), 797-817. 132 effect sizes from 76 articles, about 21,000 participants. Three things the storyteller controls raise transportation: **identifiable characters, an imaginable plot (rich mental imagery) and verisimilitude (events that seem possible and plausible)**. On the listener's side, familiarity with the genre, attention, general transportability, education and being female also raise it. The paper extends Green & Brock's Transportation-Imagery Model. — [City Research Online record](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/); [accepted manuscript PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/18870/1/Extended%20Transportation-Imagery%20Model%20CRO.pdf); [OnFiction research bulletin](https://www.onfiction.ca/2014/02/research-bulletin-summarizing-over.html)
- **Braddock & Dillard (2016) meta-analysis [Strong].** *Communication Monographs* 83(4), 446-467. Exposure to a narrative is linked to narrative-consistent beliefs (k=37, N=7,376, r=.17), attitudes (k=40, N=7,132, r=.19), intentions (k=28, N=5,211, r=.17) and behaviours (k=5, N=978, r=.23; few studies behind this one). Medium and research design did not change the effect. Results on fiction versus nonfiction were mixed, and secondary write-ups contradict each other on which way they lean. — [Decision Lab summary](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/society/can-stories-change-us); [Penn State Pure listing of a related health-narrative meta-analysis](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/). A separate health-communication meta-analysis of 25 studies found a smaller effect (r=.063), with a different scope. — [Penn State Pure](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/impact-of-narratives-on-persuasion-in-health-communication-a-meta/)
- **Mar et al. (2021) meta-analysis [Strong, with caveats].** Across experiments that compared the two text types, narrative texts were easier to understand and easier to recall than expository texts. The authors note that earlier evidence was mixed and that topic familiarity may explain part of the gap. A later small child study that held topic constant found no difference between genres. — [Mar et al., *Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts* (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8219577/); [TES summary](https://www.tes.com/magazine/teaching-learning/general/narrative-led-writing-can-help-students-learn); [EasyChair preprint](https://easychair-www.easychair.org/publications/preprint/8l7d)
- **Speaker-listener neural coupling (Stephens, Silbert & Hasson 2010, PNAS) [Moderate].** In fMRI, a listener's brain activity was coupled to a spoken storyteller's activity in space and time. The more anticipatory coupling, the better the listener understood. The coupling disappeared when listeners heard a language they did not understand. Small sample (about 11 listeners, one speaker). — [Hasson Lab publication page](https://hassonlab.princeton.edu/publications/speaker%E2%80%93listener-neural-coupling-underlies-successful-communication); [PMC2922522](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2922522/); [Discover Magazine](https://discovermagazine.com/mind/study-the-brains-of-storytellers-and-their-listeners-actually-sync-up)
- **Tannen, *Talking Voices* (1989; 2nd ed. 2007) [Moderate].** Ordinary conversation is full of strategies once thought "quintessentially literary." Tannen calls them *involvement strategies* because they "reflect and simultaneously create interpersonal involvement." The three she analyses are **repetition, constructed dialogue and imagery (detail)**. She replaces "reported speech" with **constructed dialogue**: dialogue in a told story is always the teller's creation, not a recording, and that is why it works. Involvement comes through sound (rhythm) and through sense (the listener's part in making meaning). — [Cambridge chapter "Involvement in discourse"](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/talking-voices/involvement-in-discourse/81368A247A655563A8818A241E8FD098); [Cambridge introduction](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/talking-voices/introduction-to-first-edition/1CDBB802A245D11A87C69163DBB253E0); [Tannen's site](https://deborahtannen.com/talking-voices); [Wikiversity review](https://en.wikiversity.org/wiki/Book_Reviews/Reflections_on_Tannen)
- **Vulnerability: the "beautiful mess effect" (Bruk, Scholl & Bless 2018, JPSP 115(2)) [Moderate to strong: seven studies].** People rate showing vulnerability (admitting a mistake, confessing feelings, asking for help, revealing something personal) more positively when someone else does it than when they do it themselves. The explanation is construal level: we see our own vulnerability concretely, and others' more abstractly and positively. A commentary cautions that this measures a gap between self and other, not proof that audiences will judge you kindly. — [Mannheim repository record](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48455); [PMC follow-up on self-compassion, 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9178778/); [Big Think](https://bigthink.com/personal-growth/the-beautiful-mess-effect-other-people-view-our-vulnerability-more-positively-than-we-do/); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/social-influence/202110/showing-vulnerability-may-be-less-risky-you-think)
- **Self-disclosure and liking (Collins & Miller 1994, *Psychological Bulletin* 116(3), 457-475) [Strong].** The meta-analysis separates three effects: people who disclose are liked more, people disclose more to those they like, and people like those they have disclosed to. More intimate disclosure tends to be liked more. Exact effect sizes are not taken from the original here. — [Collins & Miller 1994 (PDF, UCSB lab)](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/collins/nancy/UCSB_Close_Relationships_Lab/Publications_files/Collins%20and%20Miller,%201994.pdf); [Save My Exams revision note (secondary)](https://savemyexams.com/dp/psychology/ib/17/sl/revision-notes/human-relationships/personal-relationships-role-of-communication-/two-key-studies-of-communication-in-personal-relationships-)
- **Stakes [Practical].** The Moth: "a story without stakes is an essay." — [Nieman Storyboard, "How to tell a story: The Moth"](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=893)
- **Emotion and honesty [Practical].** Moth guidance: tell what you actually knew and felt at the time. Holding back emotion creates distance from the audience. — [Reader notes on The Moth's *How to Tell a Story*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/59000869-how-to-tell-a-story/7552515-erhan?page=2)

### Inferences
- van Laer's three levers translate directly into coaching. **Identifiable character:** say who you were and what you wanted. **Imaginable plot:** concrete sensory detail, a place and an object. **Verisimilitude:** specific true details instead of generalities. These have the strongest evidence of any engagement advice in this file.
- Tannen's three involvement strategies are measurable in a transcript: count lines of direct speech ("and she goes, ..."), deliberate repetition, and concrete nouns. An app could detect them and prompt for them.
- The beautiful mess effect gives evidence-based reassurance for anxious tellers: admitting a mistake or fear on stage tends to be judged more kindly than the teller expects.
- Surprise and humour come up in practitioner sources (the Moth book has a humour chapter), but I found no oral-storytelling-specific study. Treat them as practical.

### Gaps
- I found no study isolating constructed dialogue, as opposed to indirect speech, as a cause of listener engagement in oral personal stories. Tannen's evidence is discourse analysis of real talk, not an experiment.
- I did not get exact pooled effect sizes for Mar et al. (2021) or Collins & Miller (1994) because full-text fetches were blocked.
- I did not verify a humour-specific or surprise-specific study for personal stories.

## Openings and endings

### Takeaway
Advice on openings is almost all practitioner wisdom. It agrees strongly: start as close to the change as possible, in a scene, with the stakes clear, and make the opening the opposite of the ending. Labov gives modest linguistic grounding: oral narratives often open with an abstract and close with a coda that bridges back to the present. Advice on endings agrees too: end on the change in the teller and what it means now, and don't trail off.

### Cited Findings
- **Start close to the end [Practical].** Dicks moved the opening of his rental-car story from a Connecticut airport to the Alamo counter in Boca Raton, cutting scenes that weren't needed, because "the ending defines the opening." Simplifying keeps the listener from "stepping out of the river of words." The advice echoes Vonnegut ("start as close to the end as possible"). — [Shortform blog on Storyworthy](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=136455); [Art of Manliness](https://www.artofmanliness.com/people/social-skills/how-to-tell-better-stories/); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew)
- **Open on the opposite of the ending [Practical].** If the story ends with you feeling confident, start with a moment of doubt. — [SuperSummary](https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/); [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/storyworthy-summary-matthew-dicks)
- **Abstract and coda [Moderate].** Labov's abstract (an optional one-line summary up front, such as "Did I ever tell you about the time I nearly drowned?") and his coda (bringing the listener back to the present) are the opening and closing moves seen in natural oral narratives. — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **Ending on the change [Practical].** The spine's "And ever since then…" and Dicks's five-second moment both put the ending on the lasting change. — [NPR via WUNC](https://www.wunc.org/2026-06-23/meet-the-creator-of-the-story-spine-an-8-sentence-tool-to-create-and-analyze-stories); [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/storyworthy-summary-matthew-dicks)
- **Moth judging [Practical].** StorySLAM judges score whether the teller kept to time, kept to the theme, and had "a story that sticks," meaning one with a conflict and a resolution. — [Arts at Michigan, "Storytelling As Art"](https://artsatmichigan.umich.edu/ink/2015/02/26/6321/)

### Inferences
- A practical rule for the app: **the first sentence should be in the scene and in motion (where you were, what was about to go wrong). The last sentence should state what is different now.** Labov's coda supports a brief bridge back to the present ("And that's why I still…").
- A callback, where the ending reuses an image or line from the opening, fits Dicks's "opposite of the ending" pattern, but I found no source that tested it.

### Gaps
- I found no experiment on hooks or story openings for oral personal narratives.
- I found no sourced material specifically on callbacks. I could not reach the Moth's official tips page (themoth.org was blocked), which is the most likely primary source on endings.

## Oral vs written: how spoken stories differ

### Takeaway
Spoken personal stories differ from written ones in well-documented ways (moderate). Listeners actively shape the telling: Bavelas showed that distracted listeners make narrators tell worse stories, especially at the ending. Tellers switch into the conversational historical present to perform key events (Wolfson, Schiffrin). Repetition, constructed dialogue and imagery do the work in speech that the page does in writing (Tannen). Moth practice backs this up: tell, don't read, no notes, and know the story by heart without memorising it word for word.

### Cited Findings
- **Listeners as co-narrators (Bavelas, Coates & Johnson 2000, JPSP 79(6), 941-952) [Moderate to strong: two experiments].** In 63 dyads of strangers, narrators told their own close-call stories. Listeners gave *generic* responses (nods, "mhm") and *specific* responses (reactions tied to the content: wincing, gasping, finishing a phrase). Distracted listeners responded less, specific responses dropped most, and **narrators told their stories less well**. The authors conclude that listeners are co-narrators, both through their own specific responses and through their effect on the narrator's performance. Later work cites this as showing that distracted listeners produce less structured narratives. — [PubMed 11138763](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11138763); [PMC article citing it (Vanaken & Hermans 2020)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7886714); [Bavelas et al. 2002 on listener gaze, TalkBank PDF](https://talkbank.org/ca/access/0docs/bavelas2002.pdf)
- **Conversational historical present (Wolfson 1979, 1982; Schiffrin 1981) [Moderate].** In everyday American English, tellers switch between past and present tense ("so I walk in and he says…") as a *performance* device. Both authors treat the switching mainly as a way to separate and mark events. — [Wolfson, *A feature of performed narrative: the conversational historical present*, Language in Society](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-in-society/article/abs/feature-of-performed-narrative-the-conversational-historical-present1/6FCCC8EE23C749A384E2BEEA9AD81EFF); [Wolfson 1982 book record](https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BA01370767?l=en); [JEFUL article reviewing CHP](https://ojs.utlib.ee/index.php/jeful/article/download/jeful.2015.6.3.09/10151/14889)
- **Repetition, dialogue and imagery as oral involvement [Moderate].** See Tannen above. Repetition in particular is a normal, working feature of spoken talk, not a fault. — [Cambridge, *Talking Voices*](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/talking-voices/involvement-in-discourse/81368A247A655563A8818A241E8FD098)
- **Tell, don't read [Practical].** The Moth is "NOT a venue for readings; it is a venue for tellings. No notes, papers, or cheat sheets allowed." Know the story by heart, but don't memorise it by rote. — [Seattle's Child](https://www.seattleschild.com/moth-storytelling-challenge-at-home/); [Shepherd Express](https://shepherdexpress.com/culture/ae-feature/craving-stories-and-connections-at-the-moth-storyslam/)
- **Speak it before you write it [Practical].** One storytelling instructor has students tell their stories aloud before writing them, because writing can kill the story. Spoken telling tends to stay narrative ("something happens, then something else"). — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=893)
- **Chafe on speech and writing [Moderate, not verified here].** Chafe's work on speaking versus writing (*Integration and involvement*, 1982; "Information flow in speaking and writing," 1992) is the standard source on spoken "idea units" and involvement. I could not reach a summary of its findings. — [John Benjamins chapter listing](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/books/9789027277183-tsl.21.05cha); [Forum issue listing "Speakers and Writers Do Different Things"](https://wacclearinghouse.org/docs/comppile/archives/fforum/fforum3(1)files/fforum3(1)pp1-21.pdf)

### Inferences
- **For an app with no live listener, the Bavelas result is the most important finding in this file.** Telling a story to a silent phone removes the specific responses that help narrators, so stories may come out flatter than they would with a person. Ways to compensate: ask the user to picture a specific listener, play an animated listener that reacts at the right moments, or encourage practice with a real partner. Feedback scores shouldn't be calibrated as if a solo recording were the user's best telling.
- Repetition and switching into the present tense should not be flagged as errors in spoken-story feedback. They are documented features of skilled oral telling. Filler words and verbatim reading are the real problems.

### Gaps
- I could not verify from the full text that the distraction effect was strongest at the story's dramatic ending. My recollection is that it was, but this is unverified, so treat it as such.
- Shorter sentences and more pauses in speech than in writing are widely asserted (Chafe), but I could not source them here.

## Length: how long good short stories are, and what to cut

### Takeaway
The best benchmark for a short true story told on stage is the Moth's: five minutes, with a warning and about one minute of grace (practical, but a widely used standard). Cutting advice from practitioners agrees: start near the change, drop scenes and tangents that aren't on the arc, and keep only detail that serves the causal chain or the felt experience.

### Cited Findings
- **Moth StorySLAM [Practical/institutional].** Five minutes per teller. Tellers are signalled at five minutes, with a grace period of about one minute; some events stop the story at six. Overrunning can lower the score. Stories must be true ("fabricated people, places or events are not allowed"). No notes, props or costumes. — [Seattle's Child](https://www.seattleschild.com/moth-storytelling-challenge-at-home/); [Arts at Michigan](https://artsatmichigan.umich.edu/ink/2015/02/26/6321/); [Rich Mix, London Moth StorySLAM](https://richmix.org.uk/events/the-london-moth-storyslam/); [WWNO](https://www.wwno.org/post/join-us-moth-storyslam-tuesday-september-9)
- **Cut tangents [Practical].** Digressions that don't connect to the arc confuse listeners. — [Reader notes on The Moth's *How to Tell a Story*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/59000869-how-to-tell-a-story/7552515-erhan?page=2)
- **Cut setup by starting late [Practical].** Dicks's rental-car revision cut whole scenes by moving the start forward. — [Shortform blog](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=136455)
- **Keep some "ordinary" detail [Moderate].** Labov: non-reportable, non-causal events still convey the experience, so don't cut everything that isn't plot. — [LINGUIST List](https://linguistlist.org/issues/25/3040/)

### Inferences
- For everyday conversation, toasts and work, a short-form story probably sits at 1-3 minutes, and a stage story at about 5. This is my inference from the Moth's ceiling; I found no source for a typical conversational story length.
- A cutting order for coaching: (1) everything before the last moment of "normal," (2) any scene not linked by but/therefore, (3) background facts listeners don't need to follow the MRE. Keep one or two sensory details and the dialogue at the peak.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring how long everyday conversational stories are, or the best length for toasts or work stories.

## Common mistakes people make when telling stories

### Takeaway
Most "common mistakes" lists are practitioner opinion (practical), but each maps onto a mechanism above. **No point** is missing evaluation (Labov). **Listing events** is "and then" with no causation (but/therefore). **Too much setup** is starting too far from the change (Dicks). **No feeling** is withheld emotion, low transportation and missing involvement (Moth, van Laer, Tannen). **No stakes** turns the story into an essay (Moth). **Reading or memorising** kills the telling (Moth).

### Cited Findings
- **"And then" storytelling, a list with no causal link [Practical].** — [Wikipedia, Transition (fiction)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_(fiction)); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/04/tony-zhou-storytelling-video-essay-f-fake-therefore-but-and-then)
- **An anecdote with no moment of change is not a story [Practical].** — [Shortform summary](https://www.shortform.com/summary/storyworthy-summary-matthew-dicks)
- **No stakes [Practical].** "A story without stakes is an essay." — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=893)
- **Holding back emotion creates distance [Practical].** — [Reader notes on The Moth's *How to Tell a Story*](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/59000869-how-to-tell-a-story/7552515-erhan?page=2)
- **Tangents and digressions [Practical].** — [same Moth notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/59000869-how-to-tell-a-story/7552515-erhan?page=2)
- **Reading, or rote memorising [Practical].** — [Seattle's Child](https://www.seattleschild.com/moth-storytelling-challenge-at-home/)
- **Missing evaluation, so listeners ask "so what?" [Moderate].** Labov's evaluation is the part that answers why the story was worth telling. — [Uni Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **Not knowing the point or ending before you start [Practical].** Dicks: work out the ending and the meaning of the five-second moment first. — [Shortform blog](https://www.shortform.com/blog/?p=136462)

### Inferences
- A rubric for an AI coach, each item tied to evidence:
  1. Is there one clear moment of change? (Labov MRE / Dicks; moderate)
  2. Do the beats connect with but/therefore? (practical)
  3. Does the teller say what it meant or felt? (Labov evaluation; moderate)
  4. Are there concrete sensory details and a person to identify with? (van Laer; strong)
  5. Is there at least one line of direct speech? (Tannen; moderate)
  6. Does it start near the change and end on what is different now? (Dicks / Story Spine; practical)
  7. Is it within time? (Moth; practical)
  8. Is there an admission or something at stake for the teller? (Moth stakes; Bruk; Collins & Miller; moderate to strong)
- "Burying the punchline" (giving the outcome away early) is a real tension. Labov's abstract sometimes previews the point, yet suspense depends on evaluation coming before resolution. A sensible coaching line: preview the stakes, not the outcome.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study cataloguing what untrained tellers get wrong in oral personal stories. These lists are expert opinion.
- I did not find usable material from Kindra Hall (*Stories That Stick*) or Toastmasters' storytelling manuals within the tool budget. Kindra Hall's four components (identifiable characters, authentic emotion, a significant moment, specific details) are known from my background, unverified here. They match van Laer's levers closely and are worth confirming.
