# How people get better at telling stories out loud, and how a practice app can help them polish a specific story

Evidence grades used throughout:
- **Strong**: peer-reviewed experiments or meta-analyses with replication.
- **Moderate**: peer-reviewed but small, self-report, single-study, or only partly applicable (for example, second-language learners rather than native speakers).
- **Practical**: practitioner guidance (The Moth, Matthew Dicks, comedy workshops). Widely used, but not tested experimentally.

Research limits: themoth.org and hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de could not be reached from this session's network, so I could not open The Moth's tips page or the full text of the task-repetition meta-analysis. Several claims below rest on secondary summaries, and each one says so.

## Retelling: what happens to a story across retellings, and does retelling improve fluency and structure?

### Takeaway
Memory research shows that repeated retelling reshapes a story: it gets shorter, leaves out odd details, and drifts toward the teller's purpose and audience, so the teller's memory follows the retelling. Second-language research shows that repeating the same narrative makes speech more fluent and complex. So retelling does tighten and smooth a story, but "tighter" partly means "more selective and stylised", not "more accurate".

### Cited Findings
- **Strong (classic, recently replicated):** In Bartlett's (1932) "War of the Ghosts" studies, the story got shorter with each serial and repeated reproduction. Obscure details were dropped or rationalised, the supernatural core disappeared, and unfamiliar items were swapped for familiar ones (canoe became boat). — [Vaia summary](https://www.vaia.com/en-us/explanations/psychology/cognition/bartlett-war-of-the-ghosts); [Save My Exams summary](https://www.savemyexams.com/gcse/psychology/aqa/19/revision-notes/memory/memory-as-an-active-process/bartletts-war-of-the-ghosts-study). These are study guides, and they disagree on sample details (20 Cambridge male undergraduates versus 20 British participants).
- **Strong:** Bergman and Roediger report that Bartlett's repeated-reproduction results had not been replicated before them, and that their own test, with a one-week delay, did replicate them. — [Bergman & Roediger, "Can Bartlett's repeated reproduction experiments be replicated?"](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/can-bartletts-repeated-reproduction-experiments-be-replicated/)
- **Moderate (diary study, small, self-report):** Marsh & Tversky (2004), *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 18(5), 491–503. Participants logged their everyday retellings, mostly of recent emotional events, told to peers to inform and/or entertain. They said 61% of their retellings were distorted in some way (exaggerated, minimised, selective or added to), yet they judged only 42% as inaccurate. People do not count selective, shaped retelling as "inaccurate". A secondary source puts the sample at about 33 undergraduates. — [Marsh & Tversky PDF (Teachers College)](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/004_Marsh_Tversky_Spinningthestoriesofourlives.PDF); [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/677284)
- **Strong (experimental):** Tversky & Marsh (2000), "Biased retellings of events yield biased memories". Retelling from a perspective (for example, to make a point or to entertain) changes later memory of the event. This works through the reorganising schema behind the retelling, as well as through rehearsing specific details. — [Tversky & Marsh PDF](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/sh_Biasedretellingsofeventsyieldbiasedmemories.pdf); related work on entertaining versus accurate retellings: [Dudukovic, Marsh & Tversky PDF](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/ofentertainingversusaccurateretellingsonmemory.PDF)
- **Moderate:** Pasupathi's work treats retelling as co-constructed with listeners. Pasupathi & Hoyt (2009), *Developmental Psychology* 45(2), 558–574, stresses "the continued importance of listeners" for narrative identity in young adults. — [UNB narrative studies record](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/18038). Pasupathi & Rich (2005), *Journal of Personality* 73(4), 1051–1086, "Inattentive listening undermines self-verification in personal storytelling". — [UNB record](https://narrativestudies.lib.unb.ca/bibcite/reference/30021). I could not retrieve either abstract, so the specific effect sizes are unverified.
- **Moderate:** A related project with Pasupathi (RE.NA.ME.) reports that distracted listening hurt the emotions attached to memories, narrative coherence and emotion regulation during storytelling. — [RE.NA.ME. project page (University of Florence)](https://www.ricercanarrazione.unifi.it/vp-138-the-research-re-na-me-relationship-narrative-memory.html)
- **Practical/academic (content not verified):** Norrick's *Conversational Narrative* (2000) has a full chapter, "Retelling and Retold Stories" (pp. 67–104), plus a chapter on formulaicity and repetition. It treats retelling, disfluencies and formulaic phrasing as strategies of everyday tellers. — [John Benjamins catalog](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/cilt.203). The chapter is paywalled, so I could not confirm its specific claims about how stories change.

### Inferences
- Repeated telling naturally pushes a story toward a shorter, more schematic, more purpose-driven version. That is what polishing looks like, so the app can treat "shorter and more focused" as an expected, healthy trend.
- Because retelling for effect changes memory itself (Tversky & Marsh), an app that coaches true stories may want to keep the user's first telling or saved notes as a reference, and to frame cuts as editorial choices rather than corrections of fact.
- Pasupathi's listener findings suggest that telling a story into a void is weaker than telling it to an attentive listener. An app can partly stand in for one by reflecting back what it heard, such as the point and the key moment, rather than giving only metrics. This is an inference: no study tested app "listeners".

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring native-speaker personal-story retellings across several tellings for changes in length, evaluation or fluency. The "retold stories become tighter and more evaluative" claim rests on Bartlett, Norrick (not verified) and practitioner reports.
- I could not retrieve Pasupathi & Hoyt's specific findings.

## Task repetition and the 4/3/2 technique: effects on fluency, complexity and accuracy

### Takeaway
Repeating the same speaking task, especially a narrative, reliably improves fluency on the repeat. A meta-analysis also finds substantial gains in syntactic complexity and accuracy. 4/3/2 (the same talk given to new listeners in shrinking time) improves fluency, but the shrinking time may matter less than the repetition itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Moderate (small, foundational):** Nation (1989). Six advanced adult ESL learners gave the same talk in 4, 3 and then 2 minutes to different partners. From the first to the third delivery, fluency rose significantly and hesitations fell. The technique came from Maurice (1983) and was popularised by Nation. — [LearnLab: Fostering fluency in second language learning](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning); [Gianfranco Conti summary](https://gianfrancoconti.com/2017/03/04/)
- **Strong (controlled experiment):** de Jong & Perfetti (2011), "Fluency training in the ESL classroom: An experimental study of fluency development and proceduralization", *Language Learning* 61(2), 533–568. In the 4/3/2 task, the group that repeated the same monologue gained more fluency than the group that changed topics. A summary reports the gains carried over to later new speech, consistent with proceduralisation. That long-term claim comes from a secondary summary and should be checked against the paper. — [LearnLab project page](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning)
- **Moderate:** A Kwansei Gakuin study found 4/3/2 improved fluency, but did no better than 2/3/4 (increasing time). The benefit may come from repetition, not from time pressure. — [Kwansei Gakuin repository PDF](https://kwansei.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/30914/files/2.pdf)
- **Moderate:** A 2021 Thai public-speaking study found speaking fluency rose significantly after 4/3/2 task repetition. — [OJED article](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/OJED/article/view/247820)
- **Strong (meta-analysis; authors and year not verified):** The meta-analysis "Task repetition and L2 oral performance" finds task repetition has a substantial effect on syntactic complexity and accuracy, with smaller gains in fluency and lexical complexity. Narrative tasks and exact repetition were most effective for syntactic complexity. Longer gaps between repetitions led to smaller gains in complexity and fluency. — [Hildesheim repository record](https://hilpub.uni-hildesheim.de/handle/ubhi/19120) (could not be fetched; based on the search abstract)
- **Moderate:** Bygate (2001), as summarised in secondary sources: 48 learners did narrative and interview tasks, and repetition significantly improved fluency and complexity. In Bygate's earlier 1996 case study, the gain in accuracy was not significant. — [Indrarathne, Lancaster conference paper](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/events/laelpgconference/papers/v08/Bimali-Indrarathne.pdf)
- **Moderate:** Replications disagree on accuracy versus complexity trade-offs. One found repeated narratives improved fluency and accuracy while complexity changed least. Another found complexity gains came at the cost of accuracy. — [TESL Canada Journal article](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1185/1005); [Lancaster paper](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/events/laelpgconference/papers/v08/Bimali-Indrarathne.pdf)
- **Moderate:** Newer work examines how task repetition interacts with enjoyment of the task to affect complexity, accuracy and fluency. — [Cambridge, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/abs/interaction-between-task-repetition-and-oral-task-enjoyment-effects-on-speech-complexity-accuracy-and-fluency/D8C1FD395F5275FF3F3AC2356FB15A45); [Studies in SLA: Task repetition and L2 speech processing](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/task-repetition-and-second-language-speech-processing/0EA95A4C7D9E90CD2AB30043F84A4635)

### Inferences
- The core loop of VoiceLab (tell the same saved story again) matches the best-supported design in this literature: exact repetition of a narrative task.
- Spacing findings suggest that repeats within the same session or on nearby days produce the largest gains. A "tell it again now" button right after feedback is well-founded.
- An optional 4/3/2-style mode (same story, with a target of 3 minutes, then 2) is plausible, but the evidence says the time squeeze is not the active ingredient. Framing it as "tell it again, tighter" would fit both the repetition effect and the practitioner value of cutting.
- Caution: almost all of this evidence comes from second-language learners. For native speakers, fluency is less of a ceiling, and gains may show up mostly as fewer fillers, fewer restarts and better structure.

### Gaps
- I could not verify the meta-analysis authors, year or effect sizes. The full text was unreachable.
- I found no task-repetition study with adult native speakers telling personal stories.

## Feedback: what kind helps speakers improve, and what is the evidence on deliberate practice?

### Takeaway
The best-established framework (Hattie & Timperley) says useful feedback answers three questions: where am I going, how am I doing, and what to do next. It should aim at the task, the process and self-regulation, not at praise of the person. Video feedback improves public-speaking delivery more consistently than it reduces anxiety.

### Cited Findings
- **Strong (major review):** Hattie & Timperley (2007), "The power of feedback", *Review of Educational Research* 77(1), 81–112 (doi:10.3102/003465430298487). Feedback should answer "Where am I going?" (feed up), "How am I going?" (feed back) and "Where to next?" (feed forward). It works at four levels: task, process, self-regulation and self. Feedback aimed at the self (praise) is the least effective. — [Kau "Keep on teaching" summary](https://sola.kau.se/keeponteaching/about-feedback-feed-up-feed-back-och-feed-forward/?lang=en); [TU Delft teaching support](https://tudelft.nl/teaching-support/didactics/teach/provide-effective-feedback); [Frontiers in Education review PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.645758/pdf). The claim that self-level feedback is least effective comes from the original paper as commonly summarised. My search did not return the paper's effect sizes.
- **Moderate (n = 74, controlled):** Liu & Aryadoust (2022), EFL university students. In-class video feedback improved delivery skill and overall public-speaking competence, and reduced speaking anxiety, compared with verbal feedback only. The feedback was teacher-guided, not pure self-review. — [Liu & Aryadoust, SiLA 11(2)](https://www.altaanz.org/2_sila_11_2__liu_aryadoust.html)
- **Moderate/weak:** Studies of video self-modelling for public speaking show it improves performance, but anxiety results are inconclusive. One undergraduate thesis found only state anxiety dropped. A master's thesis found no robust change in anxiety. Small or single-case studies report reductions. — [Western University thesis](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/psych_uht/19); [Minnesota State thesis](https://cornerstone.lib.mnsu.edu/etds/30); [Bartholomay & Houlihan 2018 (single subject)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ibn/ijpsjl/v10y2018i4p1.html); [Rickards-Schlichting 2001 dissertation](https://digitalcommons.lib.uconn.edu/dissertations/AAI3038046)
- **Practical:** Comedy workshops ("Joke Lab") separate development sessions, where people bring half-finished ideas for group feedback, from performance open mics. Courses use repeated cycles of "share 1–2 jokes, get feedback, expand as homework". — [City of Sydney: The Joke Lab](https://whatson.cityofsydney.nsw.gov.au/events/the-joke-lab-workshop-your-stand-up-comedy-material); [Big Improv writer's module](https://www.bigimprov.org/service-page/stand-up-writer-s-module)

### Inferences
- Per Hattie & Timperley, each piece of VoiceLab feedback should name the goal (for example, "get to the action sooner"), say how this telling did against it ("first action at 0:48, down from 1:20"), and give one next step. Generic praise ("great story!") adds little.
- "One thing at a time" is not directly tested in the sources above, but it follows from the feed-forward principle and from task-repetition research. Learners have limited attention, so trade-offs between complexity and accuracy appear when they try to improve everything at once.
- Letting users replay their own recording, with a prompt such as "notice where you lost the thread", is supported for performance gains. The app should not promise anxiety reduction from self-review alone.

### Gaps
- I did not find a study of deliberate practice (Ericsson-style) specific to spoken storytelling or public speaking. That remains unsourced here.
- I found no direct evidence on self-assessment versus peer feedback for spoken narratives.
- Hattie & Timperley's effect sizes were not retrieved in this session.

## How expert storytellers and coaches develop a story

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree that a story needs stakes and a single moment of change. Without those it is only an anecdote. Development means finding that moment, building backward from it, cutting what does not serve it, and telling it to listeners repeatedly without notes. This is practitioner wisdom, not tested research.

### Cited Findings
- **Practical:** The Moth's directors (Bowles, Burns, Hixson, Jenness, Tellers), in *How to Tell a Story* (Crown, 2022): a story needs stakes, meaning something the teller wants, needs or dreads losing. Stakes can be internal or external. "An anecdote is ... a moment. A story has an arc. A story has stakes." Moth stories are true, first-person, told without notes or props, and time-limited. The book has a chapter called "Your very own Moth director" about self-coaching. — [Nieman Storyboard: How to tell a story, The Moth](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=893); [Blinkist summary](https://www.blinkist.com/books/how-to-tell-a-story-en); [NPR Life Kit, Storytelling 101 transcript](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/life-kit/storytelling-101)
- **Practical:** Matthew Dicks (*Storyworthy*): every story turns on a "five-second moment" of change. The moment can be small. Find it first, then find its opposite to choose the starting point, and leave out anything that does not serve the moment. — [Lenny's Newsletter interview with Matthew Dicks](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew); [Art of Manliness: How to tell better stories](https://www.artofmanliness.com/articles/how-to-tell-better-stories/); [SuperSummary plot summary](https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/)
- **Practical:** Comedians workshop material in cycles: write, try it at open mics or workshop circles, get feedback, revise punchlines and structure. — [City of Sydney: The Joke Lab](https://whatson.cityofsydney.nsw.gov.au/events/the-joke-lab-workshop-your-stand-up-comedy-material); [Meetup: South Bay Creative Comedy Group](https://www.meetup.com/South-Bay-Creative-Comedy-Group/)
- **Strong (foundational linguistics):** Labov & Waletzky (1967) describe the structure of oral personal-experience narratives as abstract, orientation, complicating action, evaluation, resolution and coda. Evaluation (why it matters, the teller's stance) is what separates a story from a list of events. Later work found evaluation spread throughout a story, and critics note not every story has all six parts. — [Summary, University of Łódź Press](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1); [Introduction to Narrative lecture PDF](https://mail.cedw.tu.edu.iq/images/lect/EngLect/elaf%20subhi/4th/Introduction_To_Narrative.pdf)

### Inferences
- The Moth's stakes and Dicks's five-second moment are practitioner versions of Labov's "evaluation". All three say a story must make clear why it matters and what changed. This convergence makes "a clear point or moment of change" the single highest-value thing for VoiceLab to coach.
- The practitioner loop (find the moment, build backward, cut, tell to listeners, repeat) maps directly onto VoiceLab's save, tell, feedback, retell cycle.

### Gaps
- I could not get The Moth's own tips page (themoth.org was unreachable) or a first-hand description of how Moth directors coach in sessions.
- I found no source describing Toastmasters' storytelling path (Pathways). This remains unsourced.
- I found no research studies of how comedians revise material, only workshop listings.

## Measurable signs that a story improved between tellings

### Takeaway
No source I found validates a set of metrics for "story improvement". The best-grounded candidates are fluency measures from task-repetition research (speech rate, pauses, hesitations, fillers) and structural markers from Labov and the practitioners (how soon the action starts, a clear point or moment of change, evaluation). Length is ambiguous: retellings naturally get shorter, which helps up to a point.

### Cited Findings
- **Strong/moderate:** Task-repetition studies measure gains as complexity, accuracy and fluency. Fluency includes speech rate and hesitations, and Nation (1989) reported significantly fewer hesitations by the third delivery. — [LearnLab](https://learnlab.org/research/wiki/index.php/Fostering_fluency_in_second_language_learning); [Lancaster paper](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/events/laelpgconference/papers/v08/Bimali-Indrarathne.pdf)
- **Strong (classic):** Retold stories get shorter and drop peripheral detail. — [Vaia: Bartlett](https://www.vaia.com/en-us/explanations/psychology/cognition/bartlett-war-of-the-ghosts); [Bergman & Roediger](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/can-bartletts-repeated-reproduction-experiments-be-replicated/)
- **Strong (structural model):** Orientation, complicating action and evaluation can be identified in a transcript. Complicating action is the minimum a narrative needs. — [Łódź Press summary](https://press.uni.lodz.pl/index.php/wul/catalog/download/342/1537/713?inline=1)
- **Practical:** Stakes, and a single moment of change, are the markers of a story rather than an anecdote. — [Nieman Storyboard](https://niemanstoryboard.org/?p=893); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew)

### Inferences
- These are worth showing a user, each as a comparison with their previous telling:
  1. Fillers and restarts per minute. Backed by fluency research, easy to see, and it reliably drops with repetition, which gives early wins.
  2. Time to the first action (seconds of orientation before the complicating action starts). Grounded in Labov's structure and practitioners' "start in the action". This is an inference, not a validated metric.
  3. Whether the point or moment of change is stated, as a yes/no or a quote back to the user. This is the highest-value structural check, from The Moth, Dicks and Labov.
  4. Total length against the user's target. Show it as neutral information, not as "shorter is better", because Bartlett-style shortening can also strip vivid detail.
- Probably not worth showing as raw scores: counts of concrete details and dialogue lines. They are hard to detect reliably, have no validated targets, and invite gaming. Better as qualitative notes ("you added the line your dad said, and it landed").
- Hattie & Timperley suggest each metric should come with a next step, not just a number.

### Gaps
- I found no study validating any of these metrics, or setting thresholds, for native-speaker personal stories.

## Finding stories: helping people discover the stories they already have

### Takeaway
The best-known practitioner method is Matthew Dicks's "Homework for Life": spend about five minutes each day writing down a one-line note of the day's most storyworthy moment. It builds a bank of small moments that can become stories. I found no research testing it.

### Cited Findings
- **Practical:** Homework for Life means spending five minutes each day picking the day's most storyworthy moment and noting only a brief snippet. Some accounts say Dicks keeps these in a spreadsheet. The moments can be small. — [SuperSummary: Storyworthy](https://www.supersummary.com/storyworthy/summary/); [Lenny's Newsletter](https://lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-tell-better-stories-matthew); [How to Work newsletter](https://howtowork.beehiiv.com/p/tell-more-and-better-stories)
- **Moderate:** Everyday retelling centres on recent emotional events told to peers, which suggests recent emotional moments are a natural source of stories. — [Marsh & Tversky 2004 PDF](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/004_Marsh_Tversky_Spinningthestoriesofourlives.PDF)
- **Practical/academic:** Norrick's catalogue of everyday story types (personal anecdotes told for humour, family stories retold to confirm membership) suggests useful prompt categories. — [Benjamins catalog](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/cilt.203)

### Inferences
- VoiceLab could offer a lightweight daily "moment of the day" capture (one line or a short voice note) that feeds a story bank. Users then promote an entry to a saved story to tell and refine. This mirrors Homework for Life.
- Prompt categories could follow Norrick's types and The Moth's stakes (for example, "a time you wanted something badly", "a family story everyone retells", "a small moment that changed your mind").

### Gaps
- I found no empirical study of Homework for Life, or of structured memory prompts for finding stories in adults.
- I did not find research on autobiographical memory cueing techniques (such as cue-word methods) in this session.
