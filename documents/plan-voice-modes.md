# Plan: voice modes (warm-ups, clear sounds, volume, pitch, tone, pausing, shadowing)

Proposed 2026-10-19 and updated the same day with the user's answers, for the user to read before anything is built. The user asked for pronunciation drills, rhythm, pitch and volume drills, tone and pausing drills, and shadowing ("copy a speaker you admire: their pace, their energy, the way they land a point"), each with a daily minute of speaking. This plan fleshes out the modes; how to build them comes later.

| The reading in step 2 | **Stays where it is for now** (the speech-in-parts reading is still step 2 of today's practice) |
| Volume drills | Yes |

## The shape

**A new group in Practice, "Your voice"**, with seven modes. Two existing drills move into it: pause drills become part of Pausing, and tricky words become part of Clear sounds. "Build a skill" keeps Lessons and Read aloud.

| Mode | In a sentence | Length |
|------|---------------|--------|
| 1. Warm-ups | Get breath, face and mouth ready to speak | 1 min |
| 2. Clear sounds | Say tricky words and lines; see which words were hard to catch | 1 min |
| 3. Volume | Speak at set levels, and hold your volume to the last word | 1 to 2 min |
| 4. Pitch and stress | See your voice rise and fall as a line; stretch your range, end low to sound sure | 1 to 2 min |
| 5. Tone | Say one line in three feelings, and hear whether they differ | 1 to 2 min |
| 6. Pausing | Pause for effect, after a question, between points; see each pause's length | 1 min |
| 7. Shadowing | Hear a phrase, say it back the same way, compare | 2 to 3 min |

Practice already has 14 rows, so this group would be the time to give each group a "More" row (the UX review's suggestion).

Each mode links to its card in Learn's "Your voice" set, and that set grows a card per mode where one is missing.

**In today's practice:**

- **Step 2 stays the speech-in-parts reading** (the user's decision).
- **The delivery group takes the voice modes in turn.** Its picks today are the week's focus drill and a pause drill. They would become the focus drill and a voice drill. The voice drill cycles through Warm-ups, Clear sounds, Volume, Pitch and stress, Tone, Pausing and Shadowing, one each time the group comes up.
- **Warm-ups are also offered just before the reading** as an optional "Warm up first" link on step 2. That puts a minute of warm-up within reach every day without moving the reading.
- **Day one is unchanged.** Free talk still comes first (decision 78).

The plan stays four steps and about the same length.

**What every mode shows afterwards** follows the existing rule: only numbers that can be measured honestly. Where the app cannot judge something (whether a take sounded warm, say), it shows what changed and plays the takes back so the user can judge. Every mode respects "I stammer" (decision 79): no fluency judgements, and Clear sounds and Shadowing say "hard to catch" rather than counting repeats.

## 1. Warm-ups

**What it trains.** Breath, a loose jaw and face, and a ready mouth, before speaking. It is presented as getting ready, not as a cure: no claims about protecting the voice.

**What you do.** A one-minute routine of three moves of about 20 seconds each: one for breath or body, one for face and mouth, and one articulation line or voice move to say aloud. A soft timer and the move's name and cue fill the screen. The shape counts down each move; a tap skips ahead. **Nothing is recorded** (the user's decision): the microphone stays off, so warm-ups are quick, private and work anywhere.

**The moves** (about 18 to start, so a routine rarely repeats within a week):

| Set | Move | Cue on screen |
|-----|------|---------------|
| Breath and body | Shoulders down | Roll your shoulders back, let them drop, unclench your jaw |
| | Low breath | Hand on your belly. In for 4, out on a long "sss" for 8 |
| | One breath count | Breathe in, then count out loud, steady, as far as is comfortable. Stop before you squeeze |
| | Sigh it out | Breathe in, then sigh out on "haaa", from high to low |
| Face and mouth | Lip trills | Blow through loose lips like a motorboat. Slide up and down |
| | Big face, small face | Stretch your face wide, then scrunch it small. Three times |
| | Tongue circles | Run your tongue around the front of your teeth, five each way |
| | Chewing hum | Chew slowly with your mouth closed, humming |
| | Jaw drop | Let your jaw hang open, then close gently. No forcing |
| Articulation (said aloud) | Tip of the tongue | "The tip of the tongue, the teeth, the lips", three times, a little faster each time |
| | Red leather | "Red leather, yellow leather" |
| | Unique New York | "Unique New York, you know you need unique New York" |
| | Copper coffee pot | "A proper copper coffee pot" |
| | Truly rural | "Truly rural, truly rural" |
| | Thistle sticks | "Six thick thistle sticks" |
| Voice | Hum to open | Hum "mmm", then open into "mah" |
| | Siren | Slide "ooo" from low to high and back, gently |
| | Ready line | Say your first line as if to the back of the room |

**Afterwards.** No numbers and no check, just "Warmed up" and the way on (back to Today's next step, or into the drill that follows). Words from Clear sounds' "words to watch" list can turn up as the articulation line, so warm-ups still follow your trouble words.

**In today's practice.** One of the delivery group's picks in turn, and an optional "Warm up first" link on the reading step. Each routine mixes one move from each set.

**What it needs.** A guided routine screen with timed moves and no microphone, and the move list as content. Nothing else: it is the simplest mode to build.

## 2. Clear sounds (pronunciation)

**What it trains.** Finishing consonants, especially at word ends, and long words people rush (the "mumbled syllables").

**What you do.** Read one list or set of lines aloud, at a steady pace. Then a short "say these again, slowly" round with just the words that were hard to catch.

**Content.**
- The four existing tricky-word lists.
- Lines full of word endings ("She asked if the tests passed last Tuesday").
- Long-word ladders ("particular, particularly, particularity").
- Sound-alike pairs.

About 15 sets to start.

**Afterwards.**
- **Hard-to-catch words.** The words the speech service did not catch, highlighted; tap to hear each one.
- **Words to watch.** A list that builds over days. Those words come back in later drills, and as warm-up lines to say aloud, so practice follows your own trouble words.

**Honest limit.** The browser's speech service works word by word, not syllable by syllable, and it mishears accents. So the app says "hard to catch", never "mispronounced", and shows no score.

**What it needs.** The reading word check, word times and tap-to-play exist. New: the second "slowly" round, and a stored "words to watch" list.

## 3. Volume

**What it trains.**
- Being heard at the back of a room without shouting.
- Holding volume to the end of a sentence.
- Using a quieter line for contrast.

**What you do.** Three short drills, one per visit:
- **Three rooms.** Say one line as if to a friend beside you, to a meeting room, then to the back of a hall.
- **To the last word.** Read three sentences and keep the last word as strong as the first. This builds on decision 77's fading-ending check.
- **Drop for effect.** Read a short passage and say the marked line noticeably quieter.

**Afterwards.**
- A loudness strip for each take.
- For "three rooms": how big each step up was, relative to your own first take ("your hall voice was about twice your friend voice"). The phone's microphone is not calibrated, so there are no decibel figures, only comparisons with yourself.
- Endings that faded, as today.

**What it needs.** Loudness is already measured ten times a second. New:
- **The browser's automatic volume levelling must be off for these drills.** Phones even out loud and soft speech by default, which would flatten the three rooms. This needs a check on real phones.
- Results that compare takes side by side.

## 4. Pitch and stress (with pitch tracking)

**What it trains.**
- Ending statements low, so they sound certain.
- A rise for a real question.
- A livelier range than a monotone.
- Stressing the word that carries the meaning.

**What you do.** Short drills, one per visit:
- **Statement or question.** "You finished the report." then "You finished the report?" Watch the ends go down, then up.
- **Land it low.** Three statements, each ending with a clear step down.
- **Stretch the range.** Read a line with too much melody on purpose, then normally. The normal take usually lands in between, livelier than before.
- **Stress the key word.** "I didn't say she took the money", six times, stressing a different word each time. The meaning changes each time.

**Afterwards.**
- **A pitch line under each take.** It is drawn relative to your own usual pitch, so every voice reads the same way, low or high.
- **A marker at each sentence end.** It shows falling, level or rising.
- **Your range on this take.** Measured in steps against your usual, with no "good" or "bad" verdict.
- **The stress drill.** For each take, which word had the biggest jump in pitch or volume.

**What it needs.** This is the new technology: pitch tracking on the device, from the microphone, a few dozen times a second, saved with the session like loudness. Nothing leaves the device. It needs a short test on real phones first (background noise, iPhone Safari, low and high voices), and a decision recorded, because it moves further from decision 5 ("numbers from the transcript only"; loudness already moved past it).

## 5. Tone

**What it trains.** Using pitch, volume and pace on purpose to carry a feeling: warm, urgent, calm, curious, proud.

**What you do.**
1. Read the line and the three feelings to try.
2. **Later, with the ElevenLabs work:** hear a model read of each feeling first. ElevenLabs' newer model takes cues such as "[excited]" or "[calm]" in the text.
3. Record the line three times, once per feeling.
4. **Guess the tone:** the app plays one of your takes at random and you pick which feeling it was meant to be. If you can tell them apart, a listener probably can too.

**Lines** (about 20 to start), for example:

| Line | Three feelings |
|------|----------------|
| "I have some news." | Excited, worried, calm |
| "Thank you all for coming." | Warm, formal, moved |
| "We need to talk about the budget." | Calm, urgent, reassuring |
| "That's an interesting idea." | Curious, doubtful, delighted |
| "We did it." | Proud, relieved, quietly amazed |

**Afterwards.**
- The three takes side by side, with pitch range, loudness and pace for each, so you see what you changed.
- Your guess score.
- A replay of all three in a row.

**Honest limit.** The app cannot tell you a take sounded warm. It shows what changed and lets you (and the guessing game) judge.

**What it needs.** Pitch tracking (mode 4), the side-by-side comparison (mode 3), and later the ElevenLabs model reads. Each model read is made once per line and feeling, and kept as a file the app ships.

## 6. Pausing

**What it trains.**
- Pausing before the point.
- Leaving a gap after a question.
- Breaks between ideas instead of an "um".

**What you do.** The existing pause drills (pause at each ‖ mark), plus three new kinds:
- **Pause for effect.** "And the winner is ‖ you." Aim for one to two seconds.
- **Question pause.** "What would you do with one extra hour a day? ‖" Let it land.
- **Between points.** A short three-point passage with a pause after each point.

**Afterwards.**
- Each mark's pause as a bar against its target ("1.4 seconds before 'you'"). Marks with no pause are shown plainly.
- Pause words in the gaps, unless hidden.

**What it needs.** Pause marks, word times and pause detection all exist. New: a pause length for each mark, and the new texts (about 10).

## 7. Shadowing

**What it trains.** Copying a speaker's pace, energy, pauses and the way they land a point. Shadowing comes from language teaching. A review finds it can help fluency and prosody (rhythm, stress and intonation), though most studies are small, and it works best when the learner is aiming at something specific. That is why each phrase comes with "what to copy": the pause, the stressed word, the drop at the end.

**What you do.**

1. **Listen.** Hear one phrase (3 to 8 seconds), with its words on screen and the thing to copy marked: the stressed word, or where the pause falls.
2. **Repeat.** Say it straight back the same way. The default is listen first, then speak; speaking along at the same time needs headphones, or the model leaks into the recording.
3. **Compare.** See your take against the model, then go on to the next phrase. After four to six phrases, try the whole passage in one go.

**Afterwards, for each phrase:**
- How long it took you and the speaker.
- Where each of you paused.
- Words that were hard to catch.
- Your loudness shape and pitch line beside the model's, where the model's audio can be analysed (see below).

**Where the model audio comes from.**

| Source | Real speaker | Can the app analyse the model's voice | Phrase timings | Works offline | Notes |
|--------|--------------|----------------------------------------|----------------|---------------|-------|
| **ElevenLabs reads** | No (a designed voice) | Yes | Exact: ElevenLabs can return timings for every character | Yes, once made and shipped | Reliable and licensed; delivery styles to match the tone drills. ElevenLabs blocks voices that approximate prominent public figures and bans impersonation, so these are never imitations of real speakers |
| **YouTube, in YouTube's own player** | Yes, any speaker | **No.** YouTube's terms allow showing videos in its embedded player but bar downloading, so the app never gets the audio | Marked by hand: a ready-made set, or you tap at each pause while it plays | No | Its player can start at a time; the app pauses it at the phrase end. Brings Google's player into the app (its privacy-enhanced mode reduces the cookies). Videos can disappear. Needs testing on iPhones |
| **Your own audio file** (a talk you recorded, a podcast you downloaded) | Yes | Yes | Found by the app at the silences | Yes | Stays on your device. You find the file yourself. The browser cannot turn a file into text, so you paste the words if you want them shown |
| **Public-domain recordings** of speeches already in the app | Yes (for example some presidential speeches) | Yes | Prepared once | Yes | Each clip's rights need checking before it ships |

**Decided: a mix of two sources.**

1. **The built-in library**, for the daily drill:
   - ElevenLabs reads of passages and lines already in the app, in two or three speaking styles (steady storyteller, energetic, calm authority).
   - Public-domain recordings of real speakers wherever their rights are clear.

   It works offline and every comparison works.
2. **A few YouTube videos the user picks**, of speakers worth copying:
   - They play in YouTube's own player.
   - Their phrases are marked by hand once (start, end, the words, and what to copy) and shipped as a small set alongside the library.
   - Comparison covers timing, pauses and words, but not the speaker's pitch or loudness, because the app never receives the audio.

Your own audio files are not planned for now; they stay in the table above as an option.

**What it needs.**
- A phrase player.
- A comparison screen.
- Pitch tracking (mode 4) for the pitch lines.
- The ElevenLabs reads with timings.
- For YouTube: the embedded player, and the hand-marked phrases for each chosen video.

## A possible order, when building starts

1. **Warm-ups**, as a mode, in the delivery picks and as "Warm up first" on the reading step. No new technology beyond the routine screen.
2. **Clear sounds and Pausing.** Mostly existing pieces.
3. **Volume**, after checking that automatic levelling can be switched off on phones.
4. **Pitch tracking** check on real phones, then **Pitch and stress**.
5. **Tone**: first with your own three takes and the guessing game, then with ElevenLabs model reads.
6. **Shadowing**: the built-in library first, then the user's chosen YouTube videos.
